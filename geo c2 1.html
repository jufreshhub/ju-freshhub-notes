Introduction to Geology and Continental Drift

Earth Evolution, Rocks & Minerals, Geological Methods, Supercontinents, and Alfred Wegener's Drift Theory

Imagine looking at a mountain, a valley or a volcano. Have you ever wondered:

  • How did the mountain form?
  • Why are there volcanoes in Ethiopia?
  • Why does Ethiopia have a Rift Valley?
  • Were Africa and other continents always in their present positions?

Geology helps us answer these questions.

1 Part 1: Introduction to Geology

1. What is geology?

Geology is an Earth science that studies the evolution of the Earth, the materials of which it is made, and the processes acting upon them.

Let's divide this definition into three parts:

  • Evolution of the Earth: How the Earth has changed throughout its history.
  • Earth materials: The substances that make up the Earth, especially rocks and minerals.
  • Processes: The natural activities that change the Earth's surface and interior.
Simple Analogy (Doctor vs. Geologist):
  • A doctor studies bones, organs and tissues; a geologist studies rocks, minerals and Earth's structures.
  • A doctor investigates how the body works and changes; a geologist investigates how the Earth works and changes.

2. Why is geology important?

One interesting thing about geology is that many geological events happened millions or even billions of years ago. Nobody was there to observe the formation of ancient mountains or the movement of continents. So, how do geologists know what happened? They investigate evidence (like finding a footprint in mud to deduce that an animal passed through).

3. How do geologists study the Earth?

Your module identifies several sources of geological evidence:

A. Rocks and Landforms

Examining rocks and landforms visible at Earth's surface (e.g., rock layers revealing deposition history).

B. Geophysics

Study of Earth's physical properties using physical methods (e.g., studying earthquake waves to investigate deep structures).

C. Geochemistry

Analysis of the chemical composition of rocks and other Earth materials (e.g., XRF analysis for rock origin).

D. Geochronology

Methods used to determine the ages of rocks and geological events (e.g., using radioactive elements).

2 Part 2: Continental Drift Theory

5. Were the continents always where they are today?

Look at the present-day world map. Africa, South America, Asia and Australia are separated by oceans. But scientists found evidence suggesting that the continents were once joined together in one huge supercontinent called Pangaea.

Over geological time, Pangaea separated into major parts:

  • Gondwanaland: The southern landmass, which included Africa.
  • Laurasia: The northern landmass.

6. Alfred Wegener's Continental Drift Theory

Alfred Wegener proposed the Continental Drift Theory, stating that continents were once joined together and subsequently drifted apart to their present positions.

Historical Note: Alfred Wegener was a German meteorologist and geophysicist.

7. Evidence supporting Continental Drift Theory

Wegener presented four principal observations:

A. Fit of the continents:

Opposing coastlines (e.g., eastern South America and western Africa) fit together like puzzle pieces.

B. Matching mountain belts & rocks:

Mountain ranges and rock types match across West Africa, North America, Greenland, and Western Europe.

C. Distribution of fossils:

Identical plant and animal fossils (such as Mesosaurus) found on now-separated continents.

D. Paleoclimates:

Ancient glacial evidence found in tropical/southern regions like India, Australia, and South Africa.

Easy Memory Technique (F–M–F–P):
• Fit of continents  |  Matching mountains/rocks  |  Fossil distribution  |  Paleoclimates

8. Important limitation of the original theory

Wegener presented evidence that continents had moved, but he could not adequately explain the physical mechanism responsible for moving them. Later, the theory of Plate Tectonics resolved this by explaining movement through lithospheric plates.
