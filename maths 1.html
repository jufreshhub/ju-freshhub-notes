Chapter 1 · Propositional Logic

Foundations of Propositional Logic

Principles of Valid Reasoning, Statements, and Truth Values

1 What is Mathematical Logic?

Courtroom Analogy: Imagine you are a judge in a courtroom. Two individuals make assertions: one presents valid reasoning supported by premises, while the other makes an assertion that does not logically follow from the evidence. To determine which deduction is sound, rigorous rules of reasoning are indispensable.
Definition: Mathematical (symbolic) logic is an analytical theory of the art of reasoning whose primary goal is to systematize, formalize, and codify the principles of valid deductive reasoning.

In classical logic, mathematical principles allow us to systematically test whether a deduction is sound or fallacious.

Classical Deductive Example:

  • All humans are mortal.
  • John is a human.
  • Therefore, John is mortal.

Objectives of Studying Mathematical Logic:

  • Distinguish valid arguments from logical fallacies.
  • Unambiguously assess whether mathematical assertions are true or false.
  • Construct compound statements systematically using symbolic operators.
  • Formulate and verify rigorous mathematical proofs.

2 What is a Proposition?

Consider the following two assertions:

  • "Jimma University is in Ethiopia." → Evaluates directly to True
  • "A triangle has four sides." → Evaluates directly to False

Because both sentences possess a definite, unambiguous truth status, both are classified as propositions.

Definition: A proposition (or statement) is a declarative sentence that has a truth value: it is either True (T) or False (F), but not both simultaneously.
The Binary Switch Analogy: A proposition functions like a binary electrical switch. It can exist in exactly one of two distinct states:
T (True) F (False)

3 Classification of Sentences

Not every grammatical sentence is a proposition. Only declarative statements that assert a truth condition qualify:

Sentence Proposition? Type / Reason
\(2\) is an even number. Yes Declarative: True
A triangle has four sides. Yes Declarative: False
\(5 + 3 = 8\) Yes Mathematical identity: True
What is your name? No Question (Interrogative)
Close the door! No Command (Imperative)
May God bless you! No Exclamation / Wish (Optative)
Crucial Exam Distinction: A proposition does not need to be true to qualify as a proposition. A sentence that is unequivocally False is still a valid proposition.

4 Must the Truth Value Be Known Immediately?

No. Whether human knowledge currently knows the truth value is irrelevant to the status of a proposition.

Mathematical Example:
"The \(1000^{\text{th}}\) digit in the decimal expansion of \(\pi\) is \(7\)."

Even if an individual does not personally know the exact digit without computation, the statement itself inherently possesses a fixed, objective truth value: it is either conclusively true or conclusively false. Therefore, it is a proposition.

Rule: Lacking immediate knowledge of the truth value does not mean the statement lacks a truth value.

5 Evaluation of Examples

  • "Every human being needs oxygen."
    Classification: Proposition (True). It makes an objective factual claim.
  • "Please bring me water."
    Classification: Not a proposition. This is an imperative command/request with no truth value.
  • "Some planets have no moons."
    Classification: Proposition (True). A declarative astronomical claim with a definite truth status.
  • "Is Jimma University beautiful?"
    Classification: Not a proposition. This is an interrogative question involving subjective opinion.

6 Examination Guidelines & Pitfalls

The P-T-F Diagnostic Test:

To determine whether an expression is a proposition in an exam, verify three conditions:

  1. Is it a complete declarative sentence?
  2. Does it assert an objective factual claim?
  3. Can it be assigned exactly one truth value—either True or False, but not both?
Common Examination Pitfalls:
  • False statements are propositions: Saying "\(2 + 2 = 5\)" is completely false, but it is unequivocally a proposition.
  • Unknown truth values are propositions: Statements concerning unverified facts with definite truth values are propositions.
  • Non-declarative sentences are never propositions: Questions (Why?), commands (Go!), and wishes (Good luck!) have no truth values.
  • Open Sentences (Predicates): An equation containing an unspecified variable, such as \(x + 3 = 7\), is an open proposition (predicate). It cannot have a truth value until a domain value is substituted for \(x\).
