Selectivity of Perception: Attention

Focus vs. Margin, Shifting Awareness, External Stimulus Characteristics, and Internal Observer States

1 Meaning of Attention, Focus & Margin

In a busy classroom filled with whispering students, scraping chairs, and birds outside, you still concentrate primarily on your teacher. This selectivity of perception operates through attention: the perceptual process that selects certain sensory inputs for inclusion in conscious awareness while filtering out others (just like focusing on your textbook while ignoring phone notifications and background sounds).

Focus (Center of Camera Image)

Events or stimuli perceived clearly and sharply (e.g., watching the player carrying the ball during a football match).

Margin (Surrounding Area)

Events or stimuli perceived dimly, vaguely, or partially (other players, crowd noise, cold feet, or nearby smoke).

Constant Shifting: Attention never stays permanently locked. When a loud crash erupts outside while you study with music, the crash shifts from margin into focus; once checked, your focus returns to studying.

2 External Factors (Stimulus Characteristics)

External factors are physical features of the stimulus itself that capture awareness. Four primary external factors operate continuously:

A. Size and Intensity

Larger, brighter, and louder stimuli attract more attention than tiny, dim, or whispered ones (e.g., giant billboard ads, bright lights, loud announcements).

B. Repetition

Stimuli occurring repeatedly catch notice (e.g., repeated spelling errors in a paragraph, repeated commercials, or someone following you repeatedly).

Exception (Habituation): If a stimulus repeats identically without variation, sensory adaptation / habituation eventually diminishes responsiveness.

C. Novelty (Newness & Contrast)

New or unusual stimuli stand out against routine backgrounds (e.g., a new student entering class, unusually bright clothing in a crowd, a strange noise in silence).

D. Movement

Moving objects draw attention faster than stationary ones (moving car vs. parked car, flying bird, waving hands)—a survival reflex triggered by potential danger.

3 Internal Factors (Observer Psychological States)

A. Set or Expectancy (Mental Readiness)

Mental preparedness for particular sensory inputs. While sleeping, a husband expecting an urgent phone call wakes to the ring, whereas his wife expecting the baby wakes to the baby's cry. Similarly, when a teacher announces “I will test you on attention tomorrow,” you instantly notice attention-related concepts.

B. Motives and Needs

People attend to stimuli capable of satisfying immediate needs: a hungry student immediately notices food advertisements (whereas a full student ignores them), a thirsty walker spots a water seller, and an exam-prepping student prioritizes exam-relevant tips.

Key Distinction: Set / Expectancy = “What am I prepared to notice?”  |  Motives / Needs = “What do I currently want or need?”
