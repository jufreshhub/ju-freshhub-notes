Work and Energy

Mechanical Work, Variable & Spring Forces, Work–Kinetic Energy Theorem, and Energy Conservation

1 What is Work in Physics?

In everyday life, work may mean studying or carrying a bag. In physics, work is done strictly when a force transfers energy to or from an object as the object undergoes displacement.

Three Conditions Required for Mechanical Work:
  • A force must act on the object.
  • The object must undergo a non-zero displacement.
  • The force must have a component parallel to the displacement.
Work Done by a Constant Force:
$$\boxed{W = Fd\cos\theta}$$
  • \(W\) = work done, measured in joules (\(\text{J}\))
  • \(F\) = constant force magnitude, measured in newtons (\(\text{N}\))
  • \(d\) = displacement magnitude, measured in metres (\(\text{m}\))
  • \(\theta\) = angle between force and displacement (\(F_\parallel = F\cos\theta \implies W = F_\parallel d\))
Positive Work (\(\theta = 0^\circ\))
\(W = +Fd\)

Force & displacement align (pushing a trolley forward).

Negative Work (\(\theta = 180^\circ\))
\(W = -Fd\)

Opposite directions; removes kinetic energy (friction).

Zero Work (\(\theta = 90^\circ\))
\(W = 0\)

Perpendicular to motion (normal force on flat floor).

Critical-Thinking Question

You carry a heavy bag horizontally at constant height. Your hand exerts an upward force, but the bag moves horizontally. How much work does your upward force do on the bag?

2 Worked Examples — Work Done by Constant Forces

Example 1 — Simple Horizontal Push

A student pushes a box with \(F = 20\text{ N}\) through \(d = 5\text{ m}\) in the same direction (\(\theta = 0^\circ\)).

Step 1: \(F = 20\text{ N},\quad d = 5\text{ m},\quad \theta = 0^\circ\)
Step 2: \(W = Fd\cos\theta = 20(5)\cos 0^\circ = \mathbf{100\text{ J}}\)
Example 2 — Pulling at an Angle

A suitcase is pulled with \(50\text{ N}\) at \(\theta = 60^\circ\) above horizontal, moving \(8\text{ m}\) horizontally.

Step 1: \(F = 50\text{ N},\quad d = 8\text{ m},\quad \theta = 60^\circ\)
Step 2: \(W = 50(8)\cos 60^\circ = 400(0.5) = \mathbf{200\text{ J}}\) (only \(25\text{ N}\) horizontal component does work)
Example 3 — Work Done by Friction

A \(10\text{ kg}\) box slides \(6\text{ m}\) while constant friction of \(15\text{ N}\) opposes motion (\(\theta = 180^\circ\)).

\(W = 15(6)\cos 180^\circ = 90(-1) = \mathbf{-90\text{ J}}\) (removes \(90\text{ J}\) of kinetic energy)
Example 4 — Several Forces Acting Together

A box moves \(4\text{ m}\) right: applied push \(30\text{ N}\) right, friction \(10\text{ N}\) left.

Step 1 (Applied force): \(W_1 = 30(4)\cos 0^\circ = +120\text{ J}\)
Step 2 (Friction): \(W_2 = 10(4)\cos 180^\circ = -40\text{ J}\)
Step 3 (Net work): \(W_{\text{net}} = W_1 + W_2 = 120 - 40 = \mathbf{80\text{ J}}\)

3 Work Done by a Variable Force (Springs & Graphs)

Hooke's Law & Spring Work:
\(F_s = -kx\)   (\(k\) = spring constant in \(\text{N/m}\), \(x\) = displacement in \(\text{m}\))
\(\boxed{W_s = \frac{1}{2}kx_i^2 - \frac{1}{2}kx_f^2} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \boxed{W_{\text{external}} = \frac{1}{2}kx_f^2 - \frac{1}{2}kx_i^2}\)
Example 5 — Stretching a Spring

A spring with \(k = 200\text{ N/m}\) is stretched slowly from \(x_i = 0\) to \(x_f = 0.10\text{ m}\):

\(W_{\text{external}} = \frac{1}{2}kx_f^2 = \frac{1}{2}(200)(0.10)^2 = 100(0.01) = \mathbf{1\text{ J}}\) (stored as elastic potential energy)

Work From a Force–Displacement Graph: Work equals the signed area under the \(F\)-versus-\(x\) curve (negative if below the \(x\)-axis). For a triangle increasing from \(0\text{ N}\) to \(10\text{ N}\) over \(4\text{ m}\): \(W = \frac{1}{2}(\text{base})(\text{height}) = \frac{1}{2}(4)(10) = \mathbf{20\text{ J}}\).

4 Energy & The Work–Kinetic Energy Theorem

A. Kinetic Energy (\(KE\))
$$\boxed{KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2}$$

Example 6: \(m = 4\text{ kg}, v = 5\text{ m/s} \implies KE = \frac{1}{2}(4)(5^2) = \mathbf{50\text{ J}}\).

Speed Squared: Double \(v \implies 4\times KE\); Triple \(v \implies 9\times KE\).

B. Gravitational Potential Energy (\(PE\))
$$\boxed{PE = mgh}$$

Example 7: \(2\text{ kg}\) book \(3\text{ m}\) above floor (\(g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\)): \(PE = 2(10)(3) = \mathbf{60\text{ J}}\).

Depends on chosen reference height, though energy changes \(\Delta PE\) do not.

Work–Kinetic Energy Theorem:

Net work done equals the change in kinetic energy (\(u\) = initial speed, \(v\) = final speed):

$$\boxed{W_{\text{net}} = \Delta KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \frac{1}{2}mu^2}$$
Example 8 — Finding Speed Using Work

A \(2\text{ kg}\) object starts from rest (\(u = 0\)) with \(W_{\text{net}} = 100\text{ J}\):

\(100 = \frac{1}{2}(2)(v^2 - 0) \implies v^2 = 100 \implies \mathbf{v = 10\text{ m/s}}\)
Example 9 — Moving Car Slowing Down

A \(1000\text{ kg}\) car at \(20\text{ m/s}\) experiences \(W_{\text{net}} = -150,000\text{ J}\) from braking:

Step 1: \(-150,000 = \frac{1}{2}(1000)(v^2 - 20^2) = 500(v^2 - 400)\)
Step 2: Divide by \(500\): \(-300 = v^2 - 400 \implies v^2 = 100 \implies \mathbf{v = 10\text{ m/s}}\)

Critical Question: Why did we use negative work for the brakes? What would a positive value mean for the car's speed?

5 Conservative & Non-Conservative Forces

A. Conservative Forces

Work depends only on initial and final positions, not on the path (e.g., gravity, elastic spring force). Around any closed path: \(\boxed{W_{\text{closed path}} = 0}\).

B. Non-Conservative Forces

Work depends on the path taken (e.g., friction, air resistance). Sliding \(10\text{ m}\) dissipates more thermal energy than sliding \(5\text{ m}\).

Simple Hands-On Experiment: Smooth vs. Rough Track

Release a small ball from a marked height down a ramp and up a second ramp:

Experiment A: Smooth TrackExperiment B: Rough Cloth Track
Little energy lost to frictionMore energy converted to thermal & sound
Ball climbs nearly to original heightBall climbs to a visibly lower height
Mechanical energy approx. conserved (Gravity)Mechanical energy decreases (Friction)

Critical Distinction: Friction never destroys energy—total energy is always conserved; it is mechanical energy (\(KE + PE\)) that decreases by converting into heat.

6 Conservation of Mechanical Energy

$$\boxed{E = KE + PE} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \boxed{KE_i + PE_i = KE_f + PE_f}$$
$$\boxed{\frac{1}{2}mu^2 + mgh_i = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 + mgh_f}$$
Example 10 — Falling Ball (\(m = 2\text{ kg}, h = 20\text{ m}, g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\))
Step 1 (Top): \(KE_i = 0,\quad PE_i = 2(10)(20) = 400\text{ J}\)
Step 2 (Ground, \(h_f = 0\)): \(400 = \frac{1}{2}(2)v^2 \implies v^2 = 400 \implies \mathbf{v = 20\text{ m/s}}\)
Example 11 — Ball Thrown Upward (\(u = 20\text{ m/s}, g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\))
Step 1: Set \(\frac{1}{2}mu^2 = mgh\) and cancel mass \(m\)
Step 2: \(\frac{1}{2}(20^2) = 10h \implies 200 = 10h \implies \mathbf{h = 20\text{ m}}\) (independent of mass!)
Example 12 — Rough Slide With Friction (\(m = 5\text{ kg}, h = 10\text{ m}, W_f = -100\text{ J}\))
Step 1 (Initial energy): \(E_i = mgh = 5(10)(10) = 500\text{ J}\)
Step 2 (Bottom, \(h_f = 0\)): \(KE_f + PE_f = E_i + W_{\text{friction}} \implies \frac{1}{2}(5)v^2 = 500 - 100\)
Step 3: \(2.5v^2 = 400 \implies v^2 = 160 \implies \mathbf{v = 12.65\text{ m/s}}\) (vs. \(14.14\text{ m/s}\) if smooth)

7 Important Exam Traps & Conceptual Checks

  • A force can act with zero work if displacement is zero.
  • A force perpendicular to displacement does zero work.
  • Work is a scalar, not a vector.
  • Negative work means the force opposes displacement.
  • Kinetic energy scales with the square of speed.
  • Potential energy depends on chosen zero reference level.
  • Work–energy theorem strictly uses net work.
  • Mechanical energy is conserved only when non-conservative work is zero.
  • A spring force is conservative even though it varies with position.
  • Friction is non-conservative, yet total energy stays conserved.
