Historical Background and Major Perspectives in Psychology

From Philosophy to Independent Science (1879), Five Early Schools, and Six Modern Perspectives

1 How Psychology Became an Independent Science

For centuries, questions about thinking, emotions, behavior, and how the mind works belonged solely to philosophy. In the late 19th century, psychology broke away to become an independent experimental science.

Wilhelm Wundt — Father of Modern Psychology

  • In 1879, established the first psychology laboratory at the University of Leipzig, Germany—marking psychology's birth as an independent science.
  • Developed objective introspection: looking inward systematically to examine conscious experiences (e.g., reporting racing heartbeat, worry, and tension when feeling afraid).
1879 · Wilhelm Wundt · Leipzig, Germany · First Psychology Laboratory

2 The Five Early Schools of Psychology

A school of thought is a system of thinking about an issue—like five teachers observing the same student and interpreting their behavior through five different lenses:

1. Structuralism — Edward Titchener

Studies the structure/elements of the mind (sensations, images, feelings) via introspection. Like dismantling a phone into battery, screen, and camera, tasting a lemon reveals sour sensation, mental image, and emotional reaction.

2. Functionalism — William James

Studies the function and adaptive purpose of the mind—how people work, play, adapt, and survive (using questionnaires, mental tests, and observation). Instead of asking what parts make up a phone, it asks what the phone does to help us (e.g., how exam fear keeps a student alert).

3. Gestalt Psychology — Max Wertheimer & Colleagues

Focuses on the whole mind (Gestalt = form, whole, configuration, pattern). Famous principle: “The whole is greater than the sum of its parts.” Like watching an entire football team collaborate rather than just one isolated player.

4. Behaviorism — John B. Watson (with Thorndike & Skinner)

Studies strictly observable and measurable behavior. Emphasizes that behavior is learned rather than inherited through conditioned responses to environmental stimuli (plus animal studies)—e.g., a student participating more after receiving praise.

5. Psychoanalysis — Sigmund Freud

Emphasizes the unconscious mind—hidden wishes, emotional conflicts, and early childhood traumas influencing adult behavior. Investigated via clinical case studies, hypnosis, and dream analysis.

The Mental Iceberg Analogy: Conscious awareness is only the small visible tip above water; the vast hidden submerged mass represents the unconscious mind.

3 The Six Modern Perspectives of Psychology

1. Psychodynamic Perspective

Originated from Freud. Focuses on unconscious motives, childhood experiences, and internal conflicts—like a hidden system error causing a visible screen malfunction.

2. Behavioral Perspective (Black Box)

Focuses on rewards, punishments, and environmental learning—studying external input and behavioral output rather than hidden internal states.

3. Humanistic Perspective

Emphasizes free will, uniqueness, values, personal growth, and self-actualization (realizing full potential—like a seed growing into a full tree).

4. Cognitive Perspective

Studies mental processes (reasoning, memory, beliefs, problem solving) like a computer processing data, plus brain electrical recording, stimulation, and metabolic tracing.

5. Biological Perspective

Studies brain structures, neurochemicals, and genetic heritage as the physical “hardware” interacting dynamically with behavior and feelings.

6. Sociocultural Perspective

Examines family, peers, groups, and culture. Just as a fish cannot live without water, human behavior cannot be separated from social context (both producers and products of culture).

Distinction & Memorization Checklist:

Do not confuse the 5 historical early schools with the 6 modern perspectives (Psychodynamic grew from Psychoanalysis; Behavioral from Behaviorism; Cognitive reclaimed mental processes).
