Meaning and Nature of Philosophy

Universal Inquiry, Etymology, Socratic Wisdom, Questioning the Apparent, and Rational Criticism

1 What Is Philosophy & Why Is It Hard to Define?

Imagine walking around Jimma University campus and seeing one student help another who fell down. Asking “Why do people help each other? Is helping always morally right? What makes an action good or bad?” goes beyond mere observation into philosophical thinking—the study of general and fundamental questions concerning existence, knowledge, truth, values, reality, and human life.

Why Philosophy Cannot Be Defined by a Single Subject Matter:

Unlike Biology (living organisms), Chemistry (matter/composition), or Geography (Earth's features), philosophy has a universal nature—dealing with fundamental questions across all existence, truth, justice, beauty, and human experience.

2 Etymology & Philosophical Wisdom

Greek Etymology: Philo (Love) + Sophia (Wisdom) = Love of Wisdom

Ancient Greek thinker Pythagoras was the first to use the term philosopher for someone driven by deep curiosity. Unlike Student A (blindly memorizing 100 pages), Student B questions why things happen and examines evidence—seeking truth rather than simply collecting facts.

Philosophical Wisdom (Socrates) vs. Technical Expertise:

A skilled surgeon possesses technical expertise, not automatically philosophical wisdom. According to Socrates, philosophical wisdom requires three habits:

  • A. Critical Habit: Examining evidence before accepting claims (e.g., questioning an unverified cure-all mixture).
  • B. Eternal Vigilance: Staying continually alert and questioning—even after passing an exam.
  • C. Reverence for Truth: Respecting truth from any source (accepting a friend's sound counterargument rather than rejecting it out of pride).

3 Questioning the Apparent & Two Sides of Philosophy

Questioning the Apparent & Socratic Wonder

Apparent means what seems obvious (the sun rising, or assumptions like “poor people simply don't work hard”). Questioning the apparent does NOT deny reality—it examines underlying assumptions and alternatives (opportunities, education, society). As Socrates stated: “Wonder is the feeling of a philosopher, and philosophy begins in wonder.”

1. Critical Side

Examining and evaluating existing answers and hidden assumptions (e.g., scrutinizing the claim “Money is the only source of happiness”).

2. Constructive Side

Formulating rationally defensible answers and reasoned positions to fundamental questions (developing a grounded view on human flourishing).
