Cognitive Learning Theory

Mental Information Processing, Latent Learning (Tolman & Honzik), and Insight Learning (“Aha!” Discovery)

1 What Is Cognitive Learning Theory?

When two students hear the same math lecture, Student A may memorize steps mechanically while Student B grasps why each step works and applies it to new problems. Cognitive learning theory emphasizes the internal mental processes behind learning: thinking, understanding, memory, attention, reasoning, problem-solving, beliefs/expectations, and decision-making.

Behavioral LearningCognitive Learning
Focuses on observable behaviorFocuses on internal mental processes
Emphasizes stimuli and responsesEmphasizes thinking and understanding
Driven by reinforcement & punishmentDriven by information processing & problem-solving

2 Latent Learning (Hidden Until Needed)

Core Idea — Learning First, Showing It Later:

Latent means hidden. Latent learning occurs without immediate behavioral demonstration or reinforcement—such as walking across campus daily without intentionally memorizing buildings, then effortlessly guiding a new student to the library when asked (or learning computer skills by watching and demonstrating them later).

Tolman & Honzik's Classic Rat Maze Experiment (1930):
Group 1 (Always Rewarded): Received food daily; steadily learned route without blind alleys.
Group 2 (Never Rewarded): Never received food; showed little route improvement.
Group 3 (Day 11 Reward): No food for 10 days; when food appeared on Day 11, immediately matched Group 1—proving hidden cognitive maps formed earlier!

3 Insight Learning (Gestalt / Perceptual Discovery)

Insight learning (also called Gestalt or perceptual learning) occurs when a person reorganizes their perception of a problem and suddenly discovers the solution—without trial-and-error conditioning. For instance, repeatedly pushing a locked door until noticing a small “PULL” sign triggers immediate insight.

Five Characteristics of Insight:
  • Sudden solution after difficulty
  • Reorganization of problem perception
  • Understanding part relationships
  • Transfer to similar new problems
  • “Aha!” experience of sudden discovery
Easy → Tricky Examples:
  • Easy: Suddenly realizing a missing item is inside your bag.
  • Moderate: Struggling with math, then spotting the right formula.
  • Tricky: A medical student connecting scattered symptoms to recognize a disease pattern.

4 Latent Learning vs. Insight Learning

Latent LearningInsight Learning
Remains hidden until needed/rewarded laterEmerges through sudden perceptual reorganization
Demonstrated by Tolman & Honzik's maze ratsMarked by the “Aha!” discovery experience
Ex: Watching a teacher solve problems for weeks, then applying it on an examEx: Struggling on a problem and suddenly seeing how its parts connect
