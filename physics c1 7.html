Components and Resolution of Vectors

Orthogonal Projections, Trigonometric Resolution, Quadrant Signs, and 3D Direction Cosines

1 Components of a Vector

The Classroom Walk Analogy: Suppose you walk diagonally from one corner of a classroom to the opposite corner. Instead of taking the diagonal path directly, you arrive at the exact same destination by walking \(4\text{ metres east}\) followed by \(3\text{ metres north}\).

Every two-dimensional vector \(\vec{A}\) can be separated into two mutually perpendicular orthogonal components:

\(\vec{A}_x\) — Horizontal Component (along the \(x\)-axis).
\(\vec{A}_y\) — Vertical Component (along the \(y\)-axis).
Figure 1: Resolving a Vector into Orthogonal Components
Vector Reconstruction & Resolution:

The process of splitting a vector into perpendicular parts is called resolving a vector. Adding the components reconstructs the original vector:

$$\boxed{\vec{A} = \vec{A}_x + \vec{A}_y}$$

2 Calculating \(x\) and \(y\) Components Using Trigonometry

When a vector of magnitude \(A\) forms an angle \(\theta\) measured counterclockwise from the positive \(x\)-axis:

  • Cosine links the angle \(\theta\) to the adjacent horizontal side (\(A_x\)).
  • Sine links the angle \(\theta\) to the opposite vertical side (\(A_y\)).
Figure 2: Right-Triangle Trigonometric Projection
Component Projection Equations:
$$\boxed{A_x = A\cos\theta} \qquad \qquad \boxed{A_y = A\sin\theta}$$

Memory Rule: Whenever \(\theta\) is measured from the \(x\)-axis, cosine yields \(x\) and sine yields \(y\).

Worked Example: Resolving a \(10\text{ N}\) Force at \(30^\circ\)

A vector has magnitude \(A = 10\text{ N}\) and makes an angle \(\theta = 30^\circ\) with the positive \(x\)-axis. Find its components:

Step 1 (\(x\)-component): \(A_x = 10\cos 30^\circ = 10(0.866) = \mathbf{8.66\text{ N}}\)
Step 2 (\(y\)-component): \(A_y = 10\sin 30^\circ = 10(0.500) = \mathbf{5.0\text{ N}}\)

3 Reconstructing Magnitude & Direction

Given orthogonal components \(A_x\) and \(A_y\), we reconstruct the magnitude \(A\) via Pythagoras' theorem and the direction angle \(\theta\) via inverse tangent:

Magnitude Formula:
$$\boxed{A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2}}$$

If \(A_x = 3\text{ m},\; A_y = 4\text{ m}\):
\(A = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = \mathbf{5\text{ m}}\)

Direction Formula:
$$\boxed{\tan\theta = \frac{A_y}{A_x} \implies \theta = \arctan\left(\frac{A_y}{A_x}\right)}$$

\(\tan\theta = \frac{4}{3} \implies \theta \approx \mathbf{53.13^\circ}\) above \(+x\)-axis.

4 Algebraic Signs Across Planar Quadrants

Figure 3: Component Signs across Quadrants
Vector Direction Quadrant \(x\)-component (\(A_x\)) \(y\)-component (\(A_y\))
Up and right Quadrant I \(+\) (Positive) \(+\) (Positive)
Up and left Quadrant II \(-\) (Negative) \(+\) (Positive)
Down and left Quadrant III \(-\) (Negative) \(-\) (Negative)
Down and right Quadrant IV \(+\) (Positive) \(-\) (Negative)
Exam Trap: A vector pointing downward and to the right has a positive \(x\)-component and a negative \(y\)-component. Always inspect direction before assigning signs.

5 3D Vector Components & Direction Cosines

In three-dimensional space, a vector incorporates a third perpendicular axis (\(z\)-direction):

3D Representation & Magnitude:
$$\vec{A} = \vec{A}_x + \vec{A}_y + \vec{A}_z \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \boxed{A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2 + A_z^2}}$$

Example: \(A_x = 2,\; A_y = 3,\; A_z = 6 \implies A = \sqrt{2^2 + 3^2 + 6^2} = \sqrt{4 + 9 + 36} = \sqrt{49} = \mathbf{7}\)

Direction Angles & Direction Cosines:

A 3D vector forms angles \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) with the positive \(x\)-, \(y\)-, and \(z\)-axes respectively:

$$\cos\alpha = \frac{A_x}{A}, \qquad \cos\beta = \frac{A_y}{A}, \qquad \cos\gamma = \frac{A_z}{A}$$
  • \(\alpha\): Angle with the positive \(x\)-axis
  • \(\beta\): Angle with the positive \(y\)-axis
  • \(\gamma\): Angle with the positive \(z\)-axis

Unit Vector Identity: \(\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1\).
