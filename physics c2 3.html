Free Fall Motion

Gravitational Acceleration (\(g\)), Sign Conventions, Vertical Launch Symmetry, and Vacuum Equivalence

1 What is Free Fall?

Definition:

Free fall is the motion of an object near Earth's surface under the influence of gravity alone. When air resistance is absent, all objects fall with a constant downward acceleration \(g\) directed toward Earth's surface:

$$\boxed{g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2}$$

What Does \(9.8\text{ m/s}^2\) Mean?

It means an object's downward velocity changes by \(9.8\text{ m/s}\) every single second (assuming no air resistance). For an object dropped from rest:

Time (\(t\))Downward Speed
0 s0 m/s
1 s9.8 m/s
2 s19.6 m/s
3 s29.4 m/s
4 s39.2 m/s
Important Distinction: Gravity (\(g\)) is an acceleration, not the object's velocity. The velocity continuously changes as time passes.

Does a Heavy Object Fall Faster Than a Light Object?

If you drop a small stone and a heavy stone simultaneously from the same height (neglecting air resistance), they reach the ground together because gravitational acceleration \(g\) is identical for both. In real air, a feather falls more slowly only because air resistance affects it much more strongly. In a vacuum, even a feather and a hammer (or bowling ball) fall side by side with identical acceleration!

2 Direction of Gravity & Sign Conventions

Gravity always points downward near Earth's surface. Whether we write \(g\) with a positive or negative sign depends purely on the coordinate axis we choose:

Case A: Upward as Positive (\(+\))
$$a = -g = -9.8\text{ m/s}^2$$
Case B: Downward as Positive (\(+\))
$$a = +g = +9.8\text{ m/s}^2$$

Exam Rule: Both choices are valid. Pick one positive direction at the start, then stay completely consistent throughout your solution!

3 The Three Common Situations of Free Fall

1. Object Dropped

Starts from rest (\(u = 0\)); downward speed increases under gravity.

2. Thrown Upward

Initial velocity is upward, while acceleration remains downward (\(a = -9.8\text{ m/s}^2\)).

3. Falling Downward

Object moves downward and speeds up under gravity. If upward is positive, both \(v\) and \(a\) are negative.

4 Applying Constant-Acceleration Equations to Free Fall

When upward is chosen as the positive direction, substitute \(a = -g\) into the four standard kinematic equations (neglecting air resistance):

1. Final Velocity $$\boxed{v = u - gt}$$
2. Vertical Displacement $$\boxed{y - y_i = ut - \frac{1}{2}gt^2}$$
3. Velocity Without Time $$\boxed{v^2 = u^2 - 2g(y - y_i)}$$
4. Average Velocity Displacement $$\boxed{y - y_i = \frac{u + v}{2}t}$$
Worked Example 1: Dropped From Rest

A stone is dropped from rest (\(u = 0\)) from a building. Find its velocity and distance fallen after \(t = 3\text{ s}\):

Velocity: \(v = 0 - (9.8)(3) = \mathbf{-29.4\text{ m/s}}\) (negative means downward; speed = \(29.4\text{ m/s}\))
Distance fallen: \(y - y_i = 0 - \frac{1}{2}(9.8)(3^2) = \mathbf{-44.1\text{ m}}\) (fallen \(44.1\text{ m}\))

5 Ball Thrown Vertically Upward & Maximum Height

A girl throws a ball vertically upward with an initial speed of \(15\text{ m/s}\) (neglecting air resistance). Find the time taken to return to her hand, its return velocity, and the maximum height reached.

Crucial Peak Concept: Moving upward, speed decreases; at maximum height, instantaneous velocity \(v = 0\); moving downward, speed increases. However, acceleration is NEVER zero at the highest point—gravity still pulls the ball downward at \(a = -9.8\text{ m/s}^2\)!
Given (Upward = \(+\)): \(u = +15\text{ m/s},\; a = -9.8\text{ m/s}^2,\; \Delta y = 0\) (returns to launch height)
Total Return Time: \(0 = 15t - \frac{1}{2}(9.8)t^2 = t(15 - 4.9t)\)
\(t = 0\) (launch) or \(15 - 4.9t = 0 \implies t = \frac{15}{4.9} \approx \mathbf{3.06\text{ s} \approx 3.1\text{ s}}\)
Return Velocity: \(v = 15 - (9.8)(3.06) \approx \mathbf{-15\text{ m/s}}\) (same speed, downward)
Maximum Height (\(v_{\text{top}} = 0\)): \(0 = 15^2 - 2(9.8)h \implies 19.6h = 225 \implies \mathbf{h = 11.48\text{ m} \approx 11.5\text{ m}}\)
Three Vertical Symmetry Results (Equal Launch & Return Height):
  • Time going up \(=\) Time coming down.
  • Speed upon return \(=\) Initial launch speed.
  • At maximum height, velocity \(= 0\), while acceleration \(= g\) downward.

6 Critical-Thinking Laboratory

Challenge your physical intuition by investigating these five conceptual questions:

Question 1 (The Highest Point): A ball is thrown vertically upward. At its highest point, its velocity is zero. If acceleration is still downward, why doesn't the ball immediately have a large downward velocity at that exact instant? Think about what velocity means at a particular instant.
Question 2 (The Two Balls): You drop a heavy metal ball and a light metal ball simultaneously from the same height in a vacuum. Which one reaches the ground first? Can you explain why mass does not determine the falling acceleration in this situation?
Question 3 (The Elevator): An elevator is moving upward but slowing down. Does its acceleration point upward or downward? Compare this with a ball thrown upward.
Question 4 (The Height Problem): A ball is thrown vertically upward at \(20\text{ m/s}\). Without calculating the exact answer yet, predict: Will its maximum height be greater than or less than the height of the \(15\text{ m/s}\) ball? Will its time to return be greater or smaller? Explain your reasoning.
Question 5 (The Air-Resistance Challenge): Why does a parachutist eventually stop increasing in speed while falling, even though gravity continues acting?
