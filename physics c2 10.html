Kepler's Laws, Satellite Motion and Weightlessness

Elliptical Orbits, Law of Equal Areas, Harmonic Periods, Orbital Speed, and Apparent Weightlessness

1 Kepler's First Law — The Law of Orbits

Law Statement:

Every planet moves around the Sun in an elliptical orbit, with the Sun located at one focus of the ellipse.

Anatomy of an Ellipse:
  • An oval-shaped closed curve with two special points called foci (singular: focus).
  • The Sun sits at one focus; the other focus is empty.
  • A circle is simply a special ellipse where both foci coincide at the exact center.
Perihelion & Aphelion:
  • Perihelion: Point in orbit closest to the Sun.
  • Aphelion: Point in orbit farthest from the Sun.

Exam Trap: The Sun is NOT at the geometric center of an elliptical orbit—it is at one focus.

2 Kepler's Second Law — The Law of Areas

Law Statement:

The line joining a planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal intervals of time:

$$\boxed{\frac{\Delta A_1}{\Delta t_1} = \frac{\Delta A_2}{\Delta t_2}} \qquad \Longrightarrow \qquad \text{If } \Delta t_1 = \Delta t_2,\; \boxed{\Delta A_1 = \Delta A_2}$$
Orbital Speed Consequence:

Near perihelion (closest to the Sun), the radius line is short, so the planet must sweep through a larger arc and move fastest to cover the same area. Near aphelion (farthest from the Sun), it moves slowest.

Worked Example 1 — Applying the Law of Areas

A planet sweeps out an area of \(4 \times 10^{10}\text{ km}^2\) in \(5\text{ days}\). How much area does it sweep out in another \(5\text{-day}\) interval?

Step 1: Equal areas are swept in equal time intervals (\(\Delta t_1 = \Delta t_2 = 5\text{ days}\)).
Step 2: \(A_2 = A_1 = \mathbf{4 \times 10^{10}\text{ km}^2}\) (independent of speed or distance!)

3 Kepler's Third Law — The Law of Harmony (Periods)

Law Statement & Equations:

The square of a planet's orbital period (\(T\)) is proportional to the cube of its average distance (\(R\), the semi-major axis) from the central body:

$$\boxed{T^2 \propto R^3} \qquad \implies \qquad \boxed{\frac{T_1^2}{R_1^3} = \frac{T_2^2}{R_2^3}}$$

Exam Condition: Both planets/satellites must orbit the exact same central mass!

Worked Example 2 — Comparing Earth (\(1\text{ AU}, 1\text{ yr}\)) and Mars (\(1.52\text{ AU}\))
Step 1: \(\frac{T_M^2}{T_E^2} = \left(\frac{R_M}{R_E}\right)^3 \implies \frac{T_M^2}{1^2} = (1.52)^3\)
Step 2: \(T_M^2 = 3.512 \implies T_M = \sqrt{3.512} \approx \mathbf{1.87\text{ years}}\) (matches observed \(1.88\text{ yr}\))
Worked Example 3 — Finding an Unknown Orbital Period

Planet 1 takes \(T_1 = 1\text{ yr}\) at \(R_1 = 2 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}\). Planet 2 orbits the same star at \(R_2 = 4 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}\). Find \(T_2\).

Step 1: Rearrange comparison formula: \(T_2 = T_1\left(\frac{R_2}{R_1}\right)^{3/2}\)
Step 2: Substitute values: \(T_2 = 1\left(\frac{4 \times 10^{11}}{2 \times 10^{11}}\right)^{3/2} = 2^{3/2} = \sqrt{8}\)
Step 3: Evaluate: \(T_2 = \sqrt{8} \approx \mathbf{2.83\text{ years}}\)

4 Satellite Motion & Orbital Speed

Natural & Artificial Satellites (Tap to zoom) Swipe left

Why Doesn't a Satellite Crash Into Earth? Gravity continuously pulls the satellite downward toward Earth, while its large horizontal velocity carries it forward. Because Earth's surface curves away beneath it at the same rate it falls, it keeps missing the ground!

Deriving Circular Orbital Speed:

Gravity provides the inward centripetal force (\(F_g = F_c\)):

\(G\frac{Mm}{r^2} = \frac{mv^2}{r} \implies \text{Cancel satellite mass } m \text{ and multiply by } r:\)
$$\boxed{v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}}$$

Where \(M\) = central planet mass, \(r\) = distance from planet's center, \(G\) = gravitational constant. Satellite mass \(m\) cancels—orbital speed is independent of satellite mass! Also, higher orbits (\(r\uparrow\)) move slower (\(v\downarrow\)).

Worked Example 4 — Calculating Orbital Speed (\(h = 400\text{ km}\))

\(M_E = 5.98 \times 10^{24}\text{ kg},\; R_E = 6.38 \times 10^6\text{ m},\; G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\)

Step 1: Convert altitude: \(h = 400\text{ km} = 4.0 \times 10^5\text{ m} = 0.4 \times 10^6\text{ m}\)
Step 2: Total orbital radius: \(r = R_E + h = 6.38 \times 10^6 + 0.4 \times 10^6 = \mathbf{6.78 \times 10^6\text{ m}}\)
Step 3: \(v = \sqrt{\frac{(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(5.98 \times 10^{24})}{6.78 \times 10^6}} = \sqrt{5.88 \times 10^7} \approx \mathbf{7.67 \times 10^3\text{ m/s}}\) (\(\approx 7.67\text{ km/s}\))
Worked Example 5 — Calculating Orbital Period
Step 1: \(T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi(6.78 \times 10^6)}{7.67 \times 10^3} \approx 5555\text{ s}\)
Step 2: In minutes: \(T = \frac{5555}{60} \approx \mathbf{92.6\text{ minutes}}\) (~15.5 orbits per day)

5 Weightlessness & Continuous Free Fall

Misconception Alert: Astronauts do NOT float because “there is no gravity in space.” Gravity is very strong at space-station altitude! Astronauts feel weightless because both they and their spacecraft are in continuous free fall around Earth, leaving zero supporting normal force (\(N = 0\)).
Worked Example 6 — Freely Falling Elevator (\(m = 60\text{ kg}\))

A \(60\text{ kg}\) student stands on a scale inside an elevator whose cable snaps (free fall, no air resistance). Find the scale reading.

Step 1: Actual gravitational weight: \(W = mg = 60(9.8) = \mathbf{588\text{ N}}\)
Step 2: Both student and scale accelerate at \(a = g \implies N = \mathbf{0\text{ N}}\) (Apparent weight is zero!)

7 Critical-Thinking Questions

  1. If the Sun is at one focus of an elliptical orbit, why isn't it at the center?
  2. Why does a planet move faster near perihelion than at aphelion?
  3. If two planets orbit the same star and one is four times farther away, how many times greater is its orbital period?
  4. Why does a satellite need horizontal velocity to remain in orbit?
  5. If gravity acts on astronauts in space, why do they float?
  6. Can an astronaut be weightless while experiencing gravitational acceleration? Explain.
