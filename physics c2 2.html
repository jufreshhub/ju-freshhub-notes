Motion with Constant Acceleration

The Four Kinematic Equations, Sign Conventions, 2D Component Independence, and Stopping Distance

1 What is Constant Acceleration?

Calculating how an object moves when its velocity changes at a uniform rate forms the foundation for free fall, projectile motion, and mechanical problem-solving. Consider a car moving along a straight road with the following recorded velocities:

Time (\(t\))Velocity (\(v\))
0 s0 m/s
1 s2 m/s
2 s4 m/s
3 s6 m/s
4 s8 m/s

Every single second, the velocity increases steadily by \(2\text{ m/s}\). Thus, the acceleration is constant: \(a = 2\text{ m/s}^2\).

Definition:

Constant acceleration means that velocity changes by equal amounts in equal intervals of time throughout the motion. Think of it like adding the exact same amount of money to your savings every day—the total grows regularly.

  • Constant velocity: Velocity does not change (\(a = 0\)).
  • Constant acceleration: Velocity changes by equal amounts in equal time intervals (\(a = \text{constant}\)).

2 The Four Kinematic Equations

SymbolPhysical QuantitySI Unit
\(u\) or \(v_i\)Initial velocitym/s
\(v\) or \(v_f\)Final velocitym/s
\(a\)Constant accelerationm/s²
\(t\)Elapsed times
\(s\) or \(\Delta x\)Displacementm
Equation 1: Final Velocity \(v = u + at\)

Intuitively: \(\text{Final velocity} = \text{Initial velocity} + \text{Change in velocity}\; (\Delta v = at)\).

Example: A motorcycle starts at \(u = 5\text{ m/s}\) and accelerates at \(a = 3\text{ m/s}^2\) for \(t = 4\text{ s}\):
\(v = 5 + (3)(4) = 5 + 12 = \mathbf{17\text{ m/s}}\)
Equation 2: Displacement via Average Velocity \(s = \frac{u + v}{2}t\)

Under uniform acceleration, average velocity equals \(v_{\text{av}} = \frac{u + v}{2}\), so \(s = v_{\text{av}} t\).

Example: A car speeds up from \(u = 10\text{ m/s}\) to \(v = 20\text{ m/s}\) in \(t = 5\text{ s}\):
\(s = \frac{10 + 20}{2} \times 5 = 15 \times 5 = \mathbf{75\text{ m}}\)
Critical-Thinking Question 2

Why are we allowed to use the average of initial and final velocities here? Would this method always work if acceleration were changing unpredictably?

Equation 3: Displacement Using Time \(s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^2\)
$$\underbrace{ut}_{\text{displacement from initial velocity}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}at^2}_{\text{additional displacement due to acceleration}}$$
Example: A ball starts at \(u = 4\text{ m/s}\) with \(a = 2\text{ m/s}^2\) for \(t = 3\text{ s}\):
\(s = (4)(3) + \frac{1}{2}(2)(3^2) = 12 + 9 = \mathbf{21\text{ m}}\)
Equation 4: Final Velocity Without Time \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\)
Example: A car starts from rest (\(u = 0\)) with \(a = 4\text{ m/s}^2\) over \(s = 50\text{ m}\):
\(v^2 = 0 + 2(4)(50) = 400 \implies v = \mathbf{20\text{ m/s}}\)

4 Direction and Algebraic Signs

Choose a positive coordinate axis (e.g., East = positive, West = negative). Suppose a car travels east at \(u = +20\text{ m/s}\) while braking with acceleration \(a = -4\text{ m/s}^2\):

After 3 Seconds:

\(v = 20 + (-4)(3) = \mathbf{+8\text{ m/s}}\)

Still moving east, but more slowly.

After Another 3 Seconds:

\(v = 8 - 4(3) = \mathbf{-4\text{ m/s}}\)

Negative sign means the car stopped and reversed direction (now moving west).

5 Two-Dimensional (2D) Motion with Constant Acceleration

Motion across a plane (such as a ball crossing a football field) is analyzed by decomposing it into independent horizontal (\(x\)-axis) and vertical (\(y\)-axis) motions:

Figure 3: Two-Dimensional Motion Resolved Along Orthogonal Axes
\(\vec{u} = u_x\hat{i} + u_y\hat{j} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \vec{a} = a_x\hat{i} + a_y\hat{j}\)
\(v_x = u_x + a_xt, \qquad v_y = u_y + a_yt\)
\(\Delta x = u_xt + \frac{1}{2}a_xt^2, \qquad \Delta y = u_yt + \frac{1}{2}a_yt^2\)
Worked Example: 2D Velocity After \(3\text{ s}\)

\(\vec{u} = (3\hat{i} + 2\hat{j})\text{ m/s}, \qquad \vec{a} = (1\hat{i} + 2\hat{j})\text{ m/s}^2, \qquad t = 3\text{ s}\)

\(v_x = 3 + (1)(3) = 6\text{ m/s} \quad | \quad v_y = 2 + (2)(3) = 8\text{ m/s}\)
\(\vec{v} = (6\hat{i} + 8\hat{j})\text{ m/s} \implies |\vec{v}| = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = \mathbf{10\text{ m/s}}\)

6 Application: Can a Jet Land Safely on the Runway?

A jet plane touches down at \(100\text{ m/s}\) and decelerates uniformly at \(5\text{ m/s}^2\) until stopping. The available runway length is \(0.8\text{ km}\) (\(800\text{ m}\)). Can it stop safely within the runway?

Given: \(u = 100\text{ m/s},\; v = 0,\; a = -5\text{ m/s}^2\)
Using \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\): \(0^2 = 100^2 + 2(-5)s \implies 0 = 10000 - 10s\)
\(10s = 10000 \implies s = 1000\text{ m} = 1\text{ km} > 0.8\text{ km}\) → Cannot stop safely within the runway!
Think Beyond the Calculation

What if the pilot wanted to stop within \(800\text{ m}\)? Would the jet need greater or smaller deceleration? Why?

7 Five Deeper Questions to Investigate

  1. If an object has constant acceleration, can its velocity become zero? Give an example.
  2. Can an object have positive acceleration and negative velocity at the same time? Explain with a real situation.
  3. Why can we study \(x\)-axis and \(y\)-axis motion independently even though the object follows one combined path?
  4. A car moving at \(30\text{ m/s}\) brakes uniformly and stops. If its initial speed were doubled, how would its stopping distance change if the braking acceleration stayed the same? Derive your reasoning.
  5. Is constant acceleration possible when an object is slowing down? Explain why acceleration does not necessarily mean increasing speed.
