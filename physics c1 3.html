Measurement Uncertainty and Significant Figures

Systematic vs. Random Errors, Instrument Uncertainty Rules, and Arithmetic Precision

1 Uncertainty in Measurement & Error Types

No physical measurement is entirely exact. Even with high-grade instruments, finite scale divisions and environmental fluctuations impose limits. For example, measuring a pencil as \(L = 15.2\text{ cm}\) does not guarantee it is \(15.200000\text{ cm}\).

Definition of Uncertainty: Uncertainty characterizes the spread of measurement results and defines the quantitative interval within which the true value of a physical quantity is expected to lie.
A. Systematic Error

Occurs when an instrument is improperly calibrated, shifting every measurement consistently too high or too low.

Scale offset (+2 kg):
Actual \(50\text{ kg} \to\) Reads \(52\text{ kg}\)
Actual \(60\text{ kg} \to\) Reads \(62\text{ kg}\)

Correction: Calibrate against a known standard.

B. Random Error

Causes repeated measurements of the exact same object to fluctuate unpredictably above and below an average value.

5 Trials of a Table:
\(120.1, 120.3, 119.9, 120.2, 120.0\text{ cm}\)

Reduction: Repeat measurements and take mean.

Systematic Error Random Error
Consistently too high or too low Fluctuates up and down around mean
Caused by zero-offset or poor calibration Caused by scale fineness & observer limits
Eliminated/reduced by calibration Reduced by repeated trials & averaging

2 Estimating & Reporting Uncertainty

Rule 1: Scale (Analog) Devices
$$\text{Uncertainty} = \frac{\text{Smallest increment}}{2}$$

Example (Meter Stick): Smallest division \(= 0.1\text{ cm}\):

\(\Delta L = \frac{0.1}{2} = \mathbf{0.05\text{ cm}}\)

Rule 2: Digital Devices
$$\text{Uncertainty} = \text{Smallest increment}$$

Example (Digital Balance): Displays \(5.7513\text{ kg}\):

\(\Delta m = \mathbf{0.0001\text{ kg}}\)

Standard Measurement Notation:
$$\boxed{x = x_{\text{best}} \pm \Delta x}$$

For instance, \(L = 5.7 \pm 0.05\text{ cm}\) indicates the true value lies within: \(5.65\text{ cm} \le L \le 5.75\text{ cm}\).

Common Exam Contrast: Given an identical smallest increment of \(2\text{ mm}\):
• Scale measuring instrument \(\implies \Delta x = \frac{2}{2} = \mathbf{1\text{ mm}}\).
• Digital measuring instrument \(\implies \Delta x = \mathbf{2\text{ mm}}\).

3 Significant Figures & Counting Rules

Significant figures (significant digits) are the meaningful digits recorded in a measured quantity that reflect the precision of the measuring instrument. For example, \(0.428\text{ m}\) carries \(3\) significant figures (\(4, 2, 8\)). Reporting meaningless extra digits (like \(0.42819667\text{ m}\)) falsely implies precision your instrument cannot capture.

Rule Principle Examples & Count
1. Non-zero Digits Every digit from \(1\) to \(9\) is significant. \(25\) (2), \(8.91\) (3), \(1234.5\) (5)
2. Captive Zeros Zeros trapped between non-zero digits count. \(105\) (3), \(1002\) (4), \(1.0005\) (5)
3. Leading Zeros Zeros locating the decimal point are NOT significant. \(0.05\) (1), \(0.0062\) (2), \(0.000098\) (2)
4. Decimal Trailing Zeros Trailing zeros after a decimal point DO count. \(4.0\) (2), \(4.00\) (3), \(4.0500\) (5)
5. Whole Number Trailing Zeros Ambiguous without scientific notation. \(300 \to 3\times10^2\) (1) vs \(3.00\times10^2\) (3)
Guided Counting Practice:
• \(0.00450\): Ignore leading zeros; count \(4, 5,\) and final decimal zero \(\implies \mathbf{3\text{ sig figs}}\).
• \(20.05\): Both zeros are captive between \(2\) and \(5 \implies \mathbf{4\text{ sig figs}}\).
• \(7.000\): All trailing zeros after decimal point count \(\implies \mathbf{4\text{ sig figs}}\).

4 Significant Figures in Arithmetic Calculations

Rule A: Multiplication & Division

Round final answer to match the fewest significant figures among all input factors.

\(2.5 \times 3.42 = 8.55\)
(\(2.5 \to 2\text{ sig figs};\; 3.42 \to 3\text{ sig figs}\))
Rounded to \(2\) sig figs: \(\mathbf{8.6}\)
Rule B: Addition & Subtraction

Round final answer to match the fewest decimal places among all input terms.

\(9.65 + 8.4 - 2.89 = 15.16\)
(\(8.4\) has only \(1\) decimal place)
Rounded to \(1\) decimal place: \(\mathbf{15.2}\)
Operation What Governs Rounding?
Multiplication (\(\times\)) & Division (\(\div\)) Fewest Significant Figures
Addition (\(+\)) & Subtraction (\(-\)) Fewest Decimal Places
