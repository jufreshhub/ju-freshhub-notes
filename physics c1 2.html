SI Units and Unit Conversions

The Seven Base SI Units, Derived Unit Combinations, and Factor-Label Dimensional Conversion

1 The International System of Units (SI)

What Does SI Mean?

SI stands for the International System of Units (from the French Système International d'Unités). It is the globally accepted scientific standard used to measure physical quantities.

Established in 1960 at the 11th General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM), SI serves as the modernized metric system.

Why a Global Standard? Imagine students in different countries measuring the exact same classroom using local, unstandardized spans or cubits. Comparing results would be chaotic. The SI system provides scientists everywhere with a unified, unambiguous measurement language.

2 The Seven Base Physical Quantities

Every physical quantity in classical and modern physics can be constructed using just seven fundamental base quantities:

Base Physical Quantity SI Base Unit Unit Symbol
Length Metre \(\text{m}\)
Mass Kilogram \(\text{kg}\)
Time Second \(\text{s}\)
Electric Current Ampere \(\text{A}\)
Thermodynamic Temperature Kelvin \(\text{K}\)
Amount of Substance Mole \(\text{mol}\)
Luminous Intensity Candela \(\text{cd}\)
Memory Strategy (Two Groups):
  • Mechanics Core (3): Length, Mass, Time
  • Specialized Core (4): Current, Temperature, Mole, Luminous Intensity
Vital Distinction:

Do not confuse a physical quantity with its unit. For \(5\text{ m}\): Quantity = Length, Unit = Metre, Measurement = 5 m.

3 Derived SI Units

A derived unit is formed algebraically by multiplying or dividing base SI units according to the defining physical equation.

Example A: Area

\(L = 4\text{ m},\, W = 3\text{ m}\)

\(A = 4\text{ m} \times 3\text{ m} = 12\text{ m}^2\)

Unit: Square metre (\(\text{m}^2\))

Example B: Speed

\(d = 100\text{ m},\, t = 20\text{ s}\)

\(v = \frac{100\text{ m}}{20\text{ s}} = 5\text{ m/s}\)

Unit: Metre per second (\(\text{m/s}\))

Example C: Density

\(m = 20\text{ kg},\, V = 4\text{ m}^3\)

\(\rho = \frac{20\text{ kg}}{4\text{ m}^3} = 5\text{ kg/m}^3\)

Unit: \(\text{kg/m}^3\)

Derived Quantity SI Derived Unit Expression in Base Units
Area\(\text{m}^2\)\(\text{m} \times \text{m}\)
Volume\(\text{m}^3\)\(\text{m} \times \text{m} \times \text{m}\)
Speed\(\text{m/s}\)\(\text{m} \div \text{s}\)
Acceleration\(\text{m/s}^2\)\(\text{m} \div \text{s}^2\)
Density\(\text{kg/m}^3\)\(\text{kg} \div \text{m}^3\)
ForceNewton (\(\text{N}\))\(\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2\)

4 Principles of Unit Conversion & Conversion Factors

Unit conversion transforms a measurement from one unit system or scale into another without changing its physical magnitude. For instance, \(1\text{ metre} = 100\text{ centimetres}\) describes the exact same physical length using two different unit scales.

Why Unit Conversion is Essential:
  • Different regions and laboratory instruments employ different scales (e.g., inches vs. metres, pounds vs. kilograms).
  • Physics equations require dimensional consistency across all substituted terms.
  • Direct quantitative comparisons are impossible unless quantities share identical units.
Definition: Conversion Factor

A conversion factor is a ratio expressing the equivalence between two distinct units. Because the numerator and denominator represent the exact same physical quantity, every conversion factor equals \(1\):

$$1\text{ m} = 100\text{ cm} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \frac{100\text{ cm}}{1\text{ m}} = 1 \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{1\text{ m}}{100\text{ cm}} = 1$$

Analogy: Like exchanging currency—you alter the denomination, not the actual financial value.

5 The Unit Cancellation Method & Worked Examples

The Golden Cancellation Rule:

Arrange the conversion factor so the unwanted unit sits in the denominator (canceling diagonally) and the desired unit sits in the numerator.

$$5\,\cancel{\text{m}} \times \frac{100\text{ cm}}{1\,\cancel{\text{m}}} = 5 \times 100\text{ cm} = \mathbf{500\text{ cm}}$$
Example 1: Kilometres to Metres

Convert \(3\text{ km}\) into metres (\(1\text{ km} = 1000\text{ m}\)):

\(3\,\cancel{\text{km}} \times \frac{1000\text{ m}}{1\,\cancel{\text{km}}} = \mathbf{3000\text{ m}}\)

From larger to smaller (\(\text{km} \to \text{m}\)): multiply by \(1000\).

Example 2: Centimetres to Metres

Convert \(500\text{ cm}\) into metres (\(1\text{ m} = 100\text{ cm}\)):

\(500\,\cancel{\text{cm}} \times \frac{1\text{ m}}{100\,\cancel{\text{cm}}} = \mathbf{5\text{ m}}\)

From smaller to larger (\(\text{cm} \to \text{m}\)): divide by \(100\).

Example 3: Cross-System Length (Inches to Metres)

Convert \(0.02\text{ inches}\) into metres, given \(1\text{ inch} = 0.0254\text{ m}\):

$$0.02\,\cancel{\text{in}} \times \frac{0.0254\text{ m}}{1\,\cancel{\text{in}}} = 0.02 \times 0.0254\text{ m} = \mathbf{0.000508\text{ m}} = \mathbf{5.08 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}}$$
Example 4: Cross-System Mass (Pounds to Kilograms)

Convert a car mass of \(2500\text{ lb}\) into kilograms, given \(1\text{ lb} = 0.4536\text{ kg}\):

$$2500\,\cancel{\text{lb}} \times \frac{0.4536\text{ kg}}{1\,\cancel{\text{lb}}} = 2500 \times 0.4536\text{ kg} = \mathbf{1134\text{ kg}}$$
Three-Step Conversion Checklist:
  1. Write down the original measurement clearly with its initial unit.
  2. Multiply by the conversion factor oriented so the initial unit cancels out.
  3. Compute the numerical value and verify that only the target unit remains.
