Newton's Laws of Motion and Applications

Inertia, Acceleration, Action-Reaction Pairs, Free-Body Diagrams, Equilibrium, and Inclined Planes

1 Newton's First Law — The Law of Inertia

Law Statement:
“Every body continues in its state of rest or uniform motion in a straight line unless it is compelled to change that state by forces impressed upon it.”

A football resting on the ground stays there until kicked; moving across a perfectly smooth surface with no net force, it continues at constant velocity. Objects resist changes in motion—this property is called inertia.

Everyday Examples of Inertia (Tap to zoom) Swipe left
  • When a bus suddenly stops, passengers lean forward as their bodies tend to keep moving.
  • A stationary football stays stationary until a force acts on it.
  • Pulling a card quickly from beneath a coin lets the coin drop straight into the glass due to rest inertia.
$$\boxed{\sum \vec{F} = 0 \implies \vec{a} = 0}$$

Zero net force does not necessarily mean zero velocity—constant velocity also satisfies \(\sum\vec{F} = 0\).

Worked Example 1 — A Book Resting on a Table

A book has mass \(m = 3\text{ kg}\) resting on a horizontal table. Find the normal force acting on it (\(g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\)).

Step 1: Identify forces acting on the book
Weight downward: \(W = mg = 3 \times 10 = 30\text{ N}\)
Normal force \(N\) acts upward.
Step 2: Apply Newton's First Law (\(\sum F_y = 0\))
Taking upward as positive: \(N - W = 0\)
\(N - 30 = 0 \implies \mathbf{N = 30\text{ N upward}}\)

2 Newton's Second Law — The Law of Acceleration

Law Statement & Equation:

The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the net force acting on it and inversely proportional to its mass:

$$\boxed{\sum \vec{F} = m\vec{a}}$$
  • \(\sum \vec{F}\) = resultant or net force (in Newtons, \(\text{N}\))
  • \(m\) = mass (in kilograms, \(\text{kg}\))
  • \(\vec{a}\) = acceleration (in \(\text{m/s}^2\))

Understanding the Proportionality (\(a \propto F_{\text{net}}\) and \(a \propto \frac{1}{m}\)): Pushing an empty cart and a heavy full cart with the same force accelerates the empty cart much more. Doubling net force doubles acceleration; doubling mass halves acceleration.

Worked Example 2 — Acceleration on a Frictionless Floor

A box of mass \(40\text{ kg}\) rests on a frictionless floor. A horizontal force of \(20\text{ N}\) is applied. Find its acceleration.

Step 1: List given and required quantities
Given: \(m = 40\text{ kg}, \quad F = 20\text{ N}\)  |  Required: \(a\)
Step 2: Rearrange Newton's Second Law (\(F = ma\))
\(a = \frac{F}{m}\)
Step 3: Substitute values and evaluate
\(a = \frac{20}{40} = \mathbf{0.5\text{ m/s}^2}\)
Worked Example 3 — Opposing Forces

A \(10\text{ kg}\) box is pulled right with \(60\text{ N}\) while friction pulls left with \(20\text{ N}\). Find its acceleration.

Step 1: Calculate net force (take right as positive)
\(F_{\text{net}} = 60 - 20 = 40\text{ N}\) (to the right)
Step 2: Apply Newton's Second Law (\(F_{\text{net}} = ma\))
\(40 = 10a\)
\(a = \frac{40}{10} = \mathbf{4\text{ m/s}^2\text{ to the right}}\)

Exam Trap: Never use the applied force alone when other forces act—always compute net force first!

Worked Example 4 — Vector Force in Two Dimensions

A \(3\text{ kg}\) object has acceleration \(\vec{a} = (2\hat{i} + 5\hat{j})\text{ m/s}^2\). Find the magnitude of the resultant force.

Step 1: Apply vector equation \(\vec{F} = m\vec{a}\)
\(\vec{F} = 3(2\hat{i} + 5\hat{j}) = (6\hat{i} + 15\hat{j})\text{ N}\)
Components: \(F_x = 6\text{ N}, \quad F_y = 15\text{ N}\)
Step 2: Calculate vector magnitude using Pythagoras
\(|\vec{F}| = \sqrt{F_x^2 + F_y^2} = \sqrt{6^2 + 15^2} = \sqrt{36 + 225} = \sqrt{261}\)
\(|\vec{F}| = \mathbf{16.16\text{ N}}\)

3 Newton's Third Law — Action and Reaction

Law Statement:
“For every action, there is always an equal and opposite reaction.”
$$\boxed{\vec{F}_{AB} = -\vec{F}_{BA}}$$

When two objects interact, their interaction forces have equal magnitudes, act in opposite directions, and strictly act on different objects.

Everyday Examples of Action–Reaction (Tap to zoom) Swipe left
  • Walking: Foot pushes ground backward; ground pushes foot forward.
  • Swimming: Swimmer pushes water backward; water pushes swimmer forward.
  • Rocket Propulsion: Rocket pushes exhaust gases backward; gases push rocket forward.
Balanced Forces (On ONE Object)

Book has \(30\text{ N}\) weight down and \(30\text{ N}\) normal force up—both act on the book, so net force cancels to \(0\).

Action–Reaction (On TWO Objects)

Your hand pushes the wall (\(100\text{ N}\)); the wall pushes your hand (\(100\text{ N}\)). Because they act on different bodies, they never cancel each other!

4 Free-Body Diagrams (FBD) — Five-Step Method

A free-body diagram (FBD) isolates one selected object and displays every external force acting directly on it:

  1. Draw a simple sketch of the physical situation.
  2. Select one object and draw only the forces acting on it.
  3. Choose suitable orthogonal \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes.
  4. Resolve angled forces into components and apply \(\sum F_x = ma_x\) and \(\sum F_y = ma_y\) separately.
  5. Solve algebraically for the unknown quantities.
Horizontal Equation: \(F - f = ma_x\)
Vertical Equation: \(N - mg = ma_y\)

5 Applications: Equilibrium & Inclined Planes

A. Static & Dynamic Equilibrium (\(\sum F_x = 0,\; \sum F_y = 0\))

Worked Example 5 — Three Ropes Supporting a \(300\text{ N}\) Cement Bag

A \(300\text{ N}\) cement bag hangs from three ropes: \(T_1\) (left rope at \(53^\circ\)), \(T_2\) (right rope at \(37^\circ\)), and \(T_3\) (vertical rope). Find all three tensions (\(\cos 37^\circ = \sin 53^\circ = 0.8,\; \cos 53^\circ = \sin 37^\circ = 0.6\)).

Step 1: Analyze the stationary hanging bag
Vertical equilibrium on bag: \(T_3 - W = 0 \implies \mathbf{T_3 = 300\text{ N}}\)
Step 2: Horizontal equilibrium at the rope junction (\(\sum F_x = 0\))
\(T_2\cos 37^\circ - T_1\cos 53^\circ = 0\)
\(0.8 T_2 = 0.6 T_1 \implies T_2 = \frac{0.6}{0.8} T_1 = 0.75 T_1 \quad \text{--- Eq. (1)}\)
Step 3: Vertical equilibrium at the junction (\(\sum F_y = 0\))
\(T_1\sin 53^\circ + T_2\sin 37^\circ - T_3 = 0\)
\(0.8 T_1 + 0.6 T_2 = 300\)
Substitute Eq. (1): \(0.8 T_1 + 0.6(0.75 T_1) = 300\)
\(0.8 T_1 + 0.45 T_1 = 300 \implies 1.25 T_1 = 300 \implies \mathbf{T_1 = 240\text{ N}}\)
Step 4: Solve for \(T_2\) using Eq. (1)
\(T_2 = 0.75(240) = \mathbf{180\text{ N}}\)  |  Final: \(T_1 = 240\text{ N}, T_2 = 180\text{ N}, T_3 = 300\text{ N}\)

Critical Thinking: Neither angled rope equals \(300\text{ N}\) because the two angled ropes share supporting the bag through their vertical components.

B. Objects on Inclined Planes

Parallel (Down) \(mg\sin\theta\)
Perpendicular \(mg\cos\theta\)
Normal Force \(N = mg\cos\theta\)
Worked Example 6 — Frictionless Inclined Plane

A \(10\text{ kg}\) block slides down a smooth plane inclined at \(\theta = 30^\circ\) (\(g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\)). Find its acceleration.

Step 1: Resolve weight parallel to the incline
\(F_\parallel = mg\sin\theta = 10(10)\sin 30^\circ = 100(0.5) = 50\text{ N}\)
Step 2: Apply Newton's Second Law (\(F_\parallel = ma\))
\(50 = 10a \implies \mathbf{a = 5\text{ m/s}^2}\)
Note: Mass cancels (\(a = g\sin\theta\)), so all objects have equal acceleration on a smooth incline!
Worked Example 7 — Inclined Plane With Friction

A \(10\text{ kg}\) block slides down a \(30^\circ\) incline with \(\mu_k = 0.2\) (\(g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\)). Find its acceleration.

Step 1: Calculate normal force perpendicular to incline
\(N = mg\cos 30^\circ = 10(10)(0.866) = 86.6\text{ N}\)
Step 2: Calculate kinetic friction opposing motion
\(f_k = \mu_k N = 0.2(86.6) = 17.32\text{ N}\)
Step 3: Calculate weight component pulling down incline
\(F_\parallel = mg\sin 30^\circ = 100(0.5) = 50\text{ N}\)
Step 4: Find net force along incline (downward = \(+\))
\(F_{\text{net}} = 50 - 17.32 = 32.68\text{ N}\)
Step 5: Solve for acceleration (\(F_{\text{net}} = ma\))
\(32.68 = 10a \implies \mathbf{a = 3.268\text{ m/s}^2}\)

C. Angled Pulling Force on a Horizontal Floor

Worked Example 8 — \(20\text{ kg}\) Box Pulled at \(30^\circ\)

A \(20\text{ kg}\) box is pulled horizontally across a floor by a \(100\text{ N}\) force at \(30^\circ\) above the horizontal (\(\mu_k = 0.2, g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\)).

Step 1: Resolve the \(100\text{ N}\) applied force
Horizontal: \(F_x = 100\cos 30^\circ = 86.6\text{ N}\)
Vertical: \(F_y = 100\sin 30^\circ = 50\text{ N}\)
Step 2: Calculate Normal Force (\(\sum F_y = 0\))
Weight \(mg = 20(10) = 200\text{ N}\)
\(N + F_y - mg = 0 \implies N + 50 - 200 = 0 \implies \mathbf{N = 150\text{ N}}\)
Step 3: Calculate kinetic friction using reduced \(N\)
\(f_k = \mu_k N = 0.2(150) = 30\text{ N}\)
Step 4: Find horizontal net force
\(F_{\text{net}} = F_x - f_k = 86.6 - 30 = 56.6\text{ N}\)
Step 5: Solve for horizontal acceleration (\(F_{\text{net}} = ma\))
\(56.6 = 20a \implies \mathbf{a = 2.83\text{ m/s}^2}\)

Exam Trap: Do not assume \(N = mg\) when a pulling force has an upward vertical component—the upward pull reduces normal force and friction!

6 Common Examination Mistakes

Common MistakeCorrect Approach
Using applied force instead of net forceAdd all acting forces as vectors
Thinking zero net force means stationaryObject may move at constant velocity
Cancelling action and reaction forcesThey act on different objects
Drawing forces acting on other bodies in FBDShow only forces acting on the selected object
Assuming \(N = mg\) everywhereCheck angled forces, inclines, & vertical acceleration
Forgetting friction on an inclined planeResolve weight and calculate \(f_k = \mu_k N\)
Adding perpendicular forces arithmeticallyUse vector components & Pythagoras

7 Critical-Thinking Challenges

  1. A truck and a bicycle experience the same net force. Which has greater acceleration, and why?
  2. A person pushes a wall with \(200\text{ N}\). Why doesn't the wall necessarily move, even though the wall pushes back with \(200\text{ N}\)?
  3. A block slides down an incline. If its mass doubles while the angle and friction coefficient stay unchanged, does its acceleration change?
  4. Can a moving object have zero net force? Give an example.
  5. A box is pulled upward at an angle across a floor. Why does the friction force decrease as the upward component of the pulling force increases?
