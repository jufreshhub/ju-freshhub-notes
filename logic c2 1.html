Arguments, Premises and Conclusions

Logic Definition, Statements vs. Non-Statements, Argument Structure, and Premise/Conclusion Indicators

1 Meaning & Benefits of Studying Logic

Definition (Greek: Logos = sentence, discourse, reason, truth, rule):

Logic is the science that evaluates arguments and studies correct reasoning—acting like a football referee applying rules and evidence to decide whether a goal is valid. Its dual aim is to evaluate others' arguments and construct sound arguments of our own.

Six Key Benefits of Logic:
  • Constructing & evaluating arguments: Build sound, fallacy-free claims and separate strong reasoning from weak claims (e.g., rejecting “Everyone believes this medicine works, so it must work”).
  • Identifying reasoning errors & clarifying language: Spot thinking mistakes and verbal confusion.
  • Defending civilized society & public policy: Guard against prejudice and assess policies rationally.

2 What Is an Argument? Statements vs. Non-Statements

In logic, an argument is NOT a quarrel or shouting match—it is a group of statements in which one or more premises claim to provide support for a conclusion. A statement is a declarative sentence that is either TRUE or FALSE (and technically, its proposition is the underlying meaning/information content).

Five Non-Statements (Cannot Be Premises/Conclusions):
  • Questions: “Would you close the window?”
  • Proposals: “Let us study together.”
  • Exclamations: “Right on!”
  • Suggestions: “I suggest that you read philosophy texts.”
  • Commands: “Give me your ID card!”

3 Premises, Conclusion & Worked Examples

Every argument requires at least one premise (supporting reason/foundation) and exactly one conclusion (main claim/destination):

Example 1 (Classic)

P1: All human beings are mortal.
P2: Abebe is a human being.
C: Abebe is mortal.

Example 2 (Everyday)

P1: Regular attendees understand better.
P2: Hana attends regularly.
C: Hana understands better.

Example 3 (Tricky Bad Argument!)

P1: All doctors are educated.
P2: Some educated people are teachers.
C: Some doctors are teachers.

Still an argument—just a bad one!

4 Three Techniques to Identify Premises & Conclusions

1. Conclusion Indicators (Signal Conclusion)

Therefore, Thus, Hence, So, Consequently, Accordingly, It follows that, As a result, We may conclude, We may infer.

Ex: “All mammals breathe air. Whales are mammals. Therefore, whales breathe air.”

2. Premise Indicators (Introduce Reasons)

Because, Since, Given that, Seeing that, As indicated by, Owing to, Inasmuch as, For the reason that.

Ex: “Avoid cheating because cheating is punishable by university regulations.”

Crucial Indicator Trap: “For the reason that” introduces a premise, whereas “For this reason” introduces a conclusion (e.g., “Cheating is punishable. For this reason, students should avoid cheating.”).
3. When No Indicator Words Appear (Find the Main Point):

In “Our country should increase the quality and quantity of its military. Ethnic conflicts are intensifying. Border conflicts are escalating. International terrorist activities are increasing,” the last three sentences support the main claim—so the first sentence is the conclusion! (Ignore unrelated background comments.)
