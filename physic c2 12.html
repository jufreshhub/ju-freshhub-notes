Power

Average Power, Instantaneous Power (\(\vec{F}\cdot\vec{v}\)), Watt & Horsepower Units, and Kilowatt-Hours (\(\text{kWh}\))

1 What is Power?

Imagine two students carrying identical \(20\text{ kg}\) bags up the exact same staircase: Student A takes \(10\text{ s}\), while Student B takes \(5\text{ s}\). Both perform the exact same amount of work, yet Student B does it faster—producing greater power.

Definition & Average Power Formula:

Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred:

$$\boxed{P = \frac{W}{t}}$$
  • \(P\) = average power (watts, \(\text{W}\))
  • \(W\) = work done (joules, \(\text{J}\))
  • \(t\) = time taken (seconds, \(\text{s}\))
SI Unit of Power (Watt):
$$\boxed{1\text{ W} = 1\text{ J/s}}$$

One watt means one joule of energy is transferred every second.

Other Common Power Units:
• Kilowatt: \(1\text{ kW} = 1000\text{ W} = 10^3\text{ W}\)
• Megawatt: \(1\text{ MW} = 1,000,000\text{ W} = 10^6\text{ W}\)
• Horsepower: \(1\text{ hp} \approx 746\text{ W}\)

2 Worked Examples — Average Power

Example 1 — Simple Power Calculation

A machine performs \(W = 600\text{ J}\) of work in \(t = 10\text{ s}\):

\(P = \frac{W}{t} = \frac{600}{10} = \mathbf{60\text{ W}}\) (transfers \(60\text{ J/s}\))
Example 2 — Climbing Stairs (\(m = 60\text{ kg}, h = 5\text{ m}, g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\))
Step 1 (Work against gravity): \(W = mgh = 60(10)(5) = 3000\text{ J}\)
Step 2 (In \(10\text{ s}\)): \(P = \frac{3000}{10} = \mathbf{300\text{ W}}\)
Step 3 (In \(5\text{ s}\)): \(P = \frac{3000}{5} = \mathbf{600\text{ W}}\) (same work, halved time → doubled power!)
Example 3 — Finding Time From Power

A \(500\text{ W}\) motor performs \(10,000\text{ J}\) of work. How long does it take?

\(t = \frac{W}{P} = \frac{10000}{500} = \mathbf{20\text{ s}}\)

3 Instantaneous Power (\(P = \vec{F} \cdot \vec{v}\))

When an instantaneous force \(\vec{F}\) acts on an object moving at velocity \(\vec{v}\) separated by angle \(\theta\):

$$\boxed{P = \vec{F}\cdot\vec{v} = Fv\cos\theta}$$
Same Direction (\(\theta = 0^\circ\)):
\(P = +Fv\) (adds energy)
Opposite (\(\theta = 180^\circ\)):
\(P = -Fv\) (removes energy)
Perpendicular (\(\theta = 90^\circ\)):
\(P = 0\) (zero power)
Example 4 — Forward Engine Power

Car moves at \(20\text{ m/s}\) with \(1500\text{ N}\) forward thrust:

\(P = 1500(20)\cos 0^\circ = \mathbf{30,000\text{ W} = 30\text{ kW}}\)

Example 5 — Braking Power

Car moves at \(15\text{ m/s}\) with \(2000\text{ N}\) braking opposing motion:

\(P = 2000(15)\cos 180^\circ = \mathbf{-30,000\text{ W}}\)

4 Energy Transfer & Kilowatt-Hour (\(\text{kWh}\))

General Form & Example 6 — Electrical Energy

Power expresses any energy transfer rate: \(P = \frac{E}{t} \implies E = Pt\). A \(200\text{ W}\) device running for \(30\text{ s}\) consumes:

\(E = 200(30) = \mathbf{6000\text{ J}}\)

Exam Alert: Kilowatt-Hour (\(\text{kWh}\)) is a Unit of ENERGY, Not Power!
$$1\text{ kWh} = (1000\text{ J/s}) \times (3600\text{ s}) = \boxed{3.6 \times 10^6\text{ J}}$$
Example 7 — Electricity Consumption

A \(2\text{ kW}\) electric heater runs for \(3\text{ hours}\):

• In kWh: \(E = 2(3) = \mathbf{6\text{ kWh}}\)
• In joules: \(E = 6(3.6 \times 10^6) = \mathbf{21.6 \times 10^6\text{ J}}\)
Example 8 — Hypothetical Electricity Cost

A \(1.5\text{ kW}\) heater runs \(4\text{ h/day}\) for \(5\text{ days}\) at \(8\text{ birr/kWh}\):

Energy: \(E = 1.5(4)(5) = 30\text{ kWh}\)
Cost: \(30 \times 8 = \mathbf{240\text{ birr}}\)

5 Speed-Squared Power Comparison & Deeper Concepts

Worked Comparison — Older Car (\(0 \to v\)) vs. Newer Car (\(0 \to 2v\))

An older car accelerates from rest to speed \(v\) in \(10\text{ s}\). A newer car of identical mass \(m\) accelerates from rest to speed \(2v\) in the same \(10\text{ s}\). Find \(\frac{P_2}{P_1}\).

Step 1 (Work–KE Theorem): \(W_1 = \frac{1}{2}mv^2\)  |  \(W_2 = \frac{1}{2}m(2v)^2 = 2mv^2\)
Step 2 (Equal time \(t = 10\text{ s}\)): \(\frac{P_2}{P_1} = \frac{W_2}{W_1} = \frac{2mv^2}{\frac{1}{2}mv^2} = \mathbf{4}\) (Ratio \(4:1\), because \(KE \propto v^2\))

Conceptual Power Checks:

Q1: Two machines lift identical objects to equal heights in \(4\text{ s}\) and \(8\text{ s}\). Neither does more work; the \(4\text{ s}\) machine has twice the average power.
Q2: A car moving at constant speed on a level road still needs positive engine power to balance friction and air resistance (zero acceleration means zero net force, not zero power).
Q3: A force can act with zero instantaneous power whenever it is strictly perpendicular to velocity (\(P = Fv\cos 90^\circ = 0\)).
Q4: A high-power machine operating very briefly can do less total work than a low-power machine running for a much longer time.
