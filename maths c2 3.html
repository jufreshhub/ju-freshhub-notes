The Set of Integers

Negative Values, Additive Inverses, Algebraic Axioms, and Order Relations

1 Motivation & Definition of Integers

While the set of natural numbers \(\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}\) serves discrete counting, it presents a fundamental algebraic limitation: it is not closed under subtraction, making it impossible to represent debts or solve elementary linear equations.

Financial Balance Problem

If you have \(5\text{ Birr}\) and spend \(8\text{ Birr}\), your balance is:

\(5 - 8 = -3 \notin \mathbb{N}\)

Algebraic Equation Problem

The elementary linear equation \(x + 1 = 0\) requires:

\(x = -1 \notin \mathbb{N}\)

Definition: The set of integers, denoted by the standard mathematical symbol \(\mathbb{Z}\) (from the German word Zahlen, meaning numbers), comprises the union of negative integers, zero, and positive integers: $$\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}$$
\(\mathbb{Z}^-\) Negative Integers \(\{\dots, -3, -2, -1\}\)
\(\{0\}\) Zero (Neutral) Neither positive nor negative
\(\mathbb{Z}^+ = \mathbb{N}\) Positive Integers \(\{1, 2, 3, \dots\}\)
Strict Inclusion Hierarchy:
$$\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{W} \subset \mathbb{Z}$$

Every natural number is an integer, but not every integer is a natural number.

2 Binary Operations on Integers

A. Addition \((+)\)

The sum of any two integers is always an integer. Sign evaluation follows exact algebraic criteria:

Same Signs:

Add absolute values; retain the shared sign.

\(5 + 3 = 8\)
\(-5 + (-3) = -8\)

Different Signs:

Subtract smaller absolute value from larger; keep sign of larger.

\(-5 + 8 = 3\)
\(-9 + 4 = -5\)

B. Subtraction \((-)\)

$$a - b = a + (-b)$$

Formal Rule: Subtraction is explicitly defined as the addition of the additive inverse (opposite).

The Double Negative Rule: Subtracting a negative integer converts directly into standard addition:
\(8 - (-3) = 8 + 3 = 11\)  |  \(5 - 9 = 5 + (-9) = -4\)

C. Multiplication \((\times)\)

Multiplying integers always generates another integer. Sign rules are universally systematic:

Factor 1 Factor 2 Result Sign Example
Positive \((+)\) Positive \((+)\) Positive \((+)\) \(4 \times 3 = 12\)
Positive \((+)\) Negative \((-)\) Negative \((-)\) \(4 \times (-3) = -12\)
Negative \((-)\) Positive \((+)\) Negative \((-)\) \((-5) \times 3 = -15\)
Negative \((-)\) Negative \((-)\) Positive \((+)\) \((-4) \times (-3) = 12\)
Product Parity Rule:
  • An even count of negative factors yields a positive product: \((-2) \times (-3) = +6\).
  • An odd count of negative factors yields a negative product: \((-2) \times (-3) \times (-4) = -24\).
Non-Closure Under Division: The quotient of two integers is not necessarily an integer. For example: $$\frac{5}{2} = 2.5 \notin \mathbb{Z}$$ Hence, integers are strictly not closed under division.

3 The 10 Algebraic Properties of Integers

Group A: Five Properties of Addition Additive Ring Structure

1. Closure: \(a + b \in \mathbb{Z}\)
\(-4 + 7 = 3 \in \mathbb{Z}\)
2. Commutative: \(a + b = b + a\)
\(4 + 9 = 9 + 4 = 13\)
3. Associative: \((a+b)+c = a+(b+c)\)
\((5+9)+8 = 5+(9+8) = 22\)
4. Additive Identity: \(a + 0 = a\)
\(0\) is the additive identity
5. Additive Inverse: \(a + (-a) = 0\)
Every integer \(a\) has a unique opposite \(-a\) such that \(4 + (-4) = 0\).

Group B: Five Properties of Multiplication Multiplicative Structure

6. Closure: \(a \times b \in \mathbb{Z}\)
\((-4) \times 5 = -20 \in \mathbb{Z}\)
7. Commutative: \(a \times b = b \times a\)
\(4 \times 7 = 7 \times 4 = 28\)
8. Associative: \((ab)c = a(bc)\)
\((3 \times 5) \times 4 = 3 \times (5 \times 4) = 60\)
9. Multiplicative Identity: \(a \times 1 = a\)
\(1\) is the multiplicative identity
10. Distributive Property: \(a(b + c) = ab + ac\)
\(4(5 + 6) = 4(5) + 4(6) = 20 + 24 = 44\)

Identity Element \(0\) \(1\)

4 Order Relations & Inequalities

On the real number line, values strictly increase from left to right. Four fundamental order axioms govern the behavior of inequalities on \(\mathbb{Z}\):

1. Transitive Property:

If \(a > b\) and \(b > c\), then \(a > c\).

Example: \(8 > 5\) and \(5 > 2 \implies 8 > 2\).

2. Addition Property of Inequality:

If \(a > b\), then \(a + c > b + c\) for any integer \(c\).

Example: \(8 > 5 \implies 8 + 3 > 5 + 3 \implies 11 > 8\).

3. Multiplication Property (Sign Sensitivity):
  • Positive Multiplier \((c > 0)\): The inequality direction is preserved: \(a > b \land c > 0 \implies ac > bc \quad (\text{e.g., } 8 > 5 \implies 8(2) > 5(2) \implies 16 > 10)\)
  • Negative Multiplier \((c < 0)\): The inequality direction strictly reverses: \(a > b \land c < 0 \implies ac < bc \quad (\text{e.g., } 8 > 5 \implies 8(-1) < 5(-1) \implies -8 < -5)\)
4. Law of Trichotomy:

For any two arbitrary integers \(a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\), exactly one of the following three mutually exclusive relations holds:

$$a > b \quad \lor \quad a < b \quad \lor \quad a = b$$

Comparing \(4\) and \(7\): since \(4 < 7\), neither \(4 > 7\) nor \(4 = 7\) can be true.
