Introduction to Physics and Physical Quantities

Nature, Matter, Energy, Measurement Standards, and Basic vs. Derived Quantities

1 What is Physics?

Every day we witness remarkable events around us: a stone falling from a tree, the Sun warming our skin, a car accelerating along a road, water flowing from a tap, and electricity illuminating a room. All of these everyday observations involve natural physical phenomena.

Definition of Physics:

Physics is the branch of natural science that investigates nature, matter, energy, and their mutual interactions through accurate quantitative measurement.

Etymology: The term originates from the ancient Greek word Physis, meaning Nature.

A. Matter

Anything possessing mass and occupying spatial volume.

Examples: Stone, water, air, textbook

B. Energy

The capacity to cause change or perform mechanical work.

Examples: Electrical, heat, light, kinetic

C. Precision

Instead of vague descriptions ("moving fast"), physics quantifies: "80 km/h".

Applications of Physics in Engineering & Technology:
Scientific Field Direct Application of Physics
Medicine X-rays, MRI, ultrasound imaging
Agriculture Soil mechanics and hydraulic irrigation technology
Engineering Machines, bridges, and load-bearing structures

Energy Photovoltaic solar panels and electromagnetic generators

2 Measurement & Standard Units

The Market Scale Analogy: Suppose you buy \(2\text{ kg}\) of sugar at a market in Jimma. The vendor places the sugar on a weighing balance against calibrated iron weights. Why? Because the seller is comparing an unknown quantity of sugar with an internationally accepted standard.
Definition of Measurement:

Measurement is the quantitative act of comparing an unknown physical quantity with a fixed, universally accepted standard.

Example: Measuring a table as \(2\text{ meters}\) compares the unknown table length against the fixed \(1\text{-meter}\) standard.

A unit is the fixed physical standard used as a common language to measure a physical quantity. Without standardized units, scientific comparison across regions would be impossible.

Physical Quantity Measured Standard SI Unit
Length Meter (\(\text{m}\))
Mass Kilogram (\(\text{kg}\))
Time Second (\(\text{s}\))
Thermodynamic Temperature Kelvin (\(\text{K}\))
Worked Identification:

A student measures a pencil and records \(15\text{ cm}\):

  • Physical Quantity: Length
  • Numerical Value: \(15\)
  • Unit: \(\text{cm}\)

Rule: A complete physical measurement requires BOTH a numerical value AND a unit.

3 Physical Quantities

Saying "Student A is tall" or "Student B is heavy" does not allow mathematical calculation. When we specify height = \(1.75\text{ m}\) and mass = \(65\text{ kg}\), we are utilizing measurable properties.

Definition:

A physical quantity is a quantifiable or assignable property ascribed to a particular phenomenon or body that can be measured and expressed numerically with a unit (e.g., Length = \(5\text{ m}\), Mass = \(10\text{ kg}\), Time = \(20\text{ s}\), Temperature = \(30\,^\circ\text{C}\)).

Statement Complete Quantitative Measurement?
"The rod is long." Length is mentioned, but no numerical measurement is given.
"The rod is 5 m long." Yes (Value = 5, Unit = m).
"The water is hot." Temperature is mentioned, but no numerical measurement is given.
"Water temperature is 80 °C." Yes (Value = 80, Unit = °C).

4 Classification: Basic vs. Derived Quantities

A. Basic (Fundamental) Physical Quantities

Definition: Quantities that cannot be expressed in terms of any other physical quantity. They serve as the independent starting building blocks of measurement (e.g., Length, Mass, Time).

Building Blocks Analogy: Constructing a house starts with raw materials like bricks, cement, and steel. You don't build a brick out of a wall. Similarly, basic quantities are the foundation from which complex quantities are built.

B. Derived Physical Quantities

Definition: Quantities that can be expressed mathematically in terms of fundamental quantities. Key standard examples include Area, Volume, and Density:

1. Area (\(A\))

Classroom: \(L = 5\text{ m},\, W = 4\text{ m}\)

\(A = L \times W\)
\(= 5 \times 4 = \mathbf{20\text{ m}^2}\)
2. Volume (\(V\))

Tank: \(2\text{ m} \times 2\text{ m} \times 3\text{ m}\)

\(V = L \times W \times H\)
\(= 2 \times 2 \times 3 = \mathbf{12\text{ m}^3}\)
3. Density (\(\rho\))

\(m = 20\text{ kg},\, V = 4\text{ m}^3\)

\(\rho = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}\)
\(= \frac{20}{4} = \mathbf{5\text{ kg/m}^3}\)
Basic (Fundamental) Quantities Derived Quantities
Length, Mass, Time Area, Volume, Density, Speed
Cannot be expressed using other quantities Expressed by combining fundamental quantities

5 How Physical Quantities are Defined

1. Operational Measurement

Specifying the direct instrument/method used to measure it:

  • Distance via a meter stick
  • Time via a stopwatch
2. Mathematical Derivation

Calculating from other measurements (e.g., Average Speed):

$$\text{Average Speed} = \frac{\text{Total Distance}}{\text{Total Time}}$$

\(100\text{ m}\) in \(20\text{ s} \implies v = \frac{100}{20} = 5\text{ m/s}\)
