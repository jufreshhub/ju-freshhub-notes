Definition, Characteristics and Principles of Learning

Relatively Permanent Behavioral Change, Nine Core Characteristics, and Eight Guiding Principles

1 Definition of Learning & Non-Learning Changes

Core Definition:

Learning is a relatively permanent change in behavior occurring as a result of experience or practice (such as practicing physics problems until mastering them).

A. Change in Behavior

Alters actions (riding a bicycle) as well as knowledge, understanding, attitudes, and skills.

B. Relatively Permanent

Lasts over time (recalling English vocabulary days later), though not necessarily permanent forever.

C. Experience or Practice

Arises from training, observation, or conversation practice—not merely growing older.

D. Not Directly Observable

Cannot be seen directly inside the brain; inferred via solving problems or explaining concepts.

2 Nine Characteristics of Learning (Yoakman & Simpson)

1. Continuous (Lifelong): From child walking to older adults mastering new technology.
2. Pervasive: Happens everywhere—family, friends, work, culture, cooking, phone use.
3. Whole Person: Involves social (cooperation), emotional (patience), and intellectual growth simultaneously.
4. Organizes Experiences: Builds new ideas onto prior knowledge (cell → tissues → organs), like building a house.
5. Responsive to Incentives: Grades, job promotion, or parent praise motivate effort.
6. Active Process: Requires participation (asking questions & practicing vs. passive listening).
7. Purposeful: Goal-directed (learning English to communicate globally, medicine for healthcare).
8. Maturation, Motivation & Practice: Writing requires muscular maturity, desire, and repetition.
9. Multifaceted: Occurs via listening, reading, observation, problem-solving, and performing lab experiments firsthand.

3 Eight Principles of Effective Learning

While characteristics describe the nature of learning, principles identify the conditions that make learning succeed:

1. Readiness: Best learned when rested, healthy, and mentally/emotionally prepared (vs. sleepy or hungry).
2. Exercise (Practice): Meaningful repetition (riding a bike, solving 20 physics problems) builds mastery.
3. Effect (Satisfaction): Pleasant encouragement strengthens learning; embarrassment or insult weakens it.
4. Primacy (First Impression): What is learned first (e.g., initial word pronunciation) sticks deeply and is hard to erase.
5. Recency (Latest Learning): What was studied most recently (Wednesday's Topic C vs. Monday's Topic A) is recalled best.
6. Intensity (Real Experience): Real anatomical heart specimens or CPR manikin practice create far stronger impressions than reading text alone.
7. Ability & Skills: Learners require foundational abilities suited to the task (swimming, math reasoning, language).
8. Freedom: Things learned freely—asking questions and exploring ideas independently—are learned best.
