Types of Arguments: Deduction and Induction

Necessity vs. Probability, Three Distinction Criteria, Five Deductive Forms, and Six Inductive Forms

1 Deduction vs. Induction: Necessity vs. Probability

Deductive Argument (Strict Necessity)

Claims that if premises are true, it is impossible for the conclusion to be false (like guaranteed falling dominoes). Examples: All humans are mortal → Abebe is human → Abebe is mortal; All philosophers are critical thinkers → Socrates is a philosopher → Socrates is a critical thinker.

Inductive Argument (Probability)

Claims that if premises are true, the conclusion is probably true, yet still possibly false (tasting 3 sweet apples in a basket). Examples: Most students passed → Hana probably passed; Most African leaders are black → Mandela was probably black.

2 Five Common Forms of Deductive Arguments

A. Argument Based on Mathematics

Arithmetic/geometric calculation: 2 oranges + 3 bananas = 5 fruits → bag contains 5 fruits. (Exception: Arguments based on statistics are usually inductive.)

B. Argument Based on Definition

Follows directly from word meaning: Kebede is a physician → therefore, Kebede is a doctor.

C. Categorical Syllogism

2 premises + 1 conclusion using all, no, some: All Egyptians are Muslims; no Muslim is Christian → no Egyptian is Christian.

D. Hypothetical Syllogism

Conditional chain: If study hard → graduate with distinction; if distinction → rewarding job → therefore, if study hard → rewarding job.

E. Disjunctive Syllogism

Either… or elimination: Rewina is either Ethiopian or Eritrean; not Eritrean → therefore, Rewina is Ethiopian.

3 Six Common Forms of Inductive Arguments

A. Prediction

Using present/past evidence to forecast the future: Dark rain clouds are forming over the highlands → it will probably rain within 24 hours.

B. Argument from Analogy

Shared similarities: Computers A & B (2012, easy access); A is fast → B is probably fast too.

D. Argument from Authority

Expert/witness claim: Professor says matter consists of quarks → probably true (experts can err).

C. Inductive Generalization

Sample → whole group: 3/4 sampled inmates in a prison → 3/4 of prison population.

E. Argument Based on Signs

“No Parking” sign posted → parking probably not allowed (sign could be misplaced).

F. Causal Inference (Cause ↔ Effect)
Cause → Effect

A bottle of water was left in a freezer overnight. Therefore, it probably froze.

Effect → Cause

Chicken tastes dry and tough. Therefore, it was probably overcooked.

4 Three Essential Exam Distinctions

  • Overlapping Inductive Forms: A causal inference predicting a future effect from a present cause is simultaneously a prediction.
  • Science Uses Both: Discovering a general law of nature is typically inductive, whereas applying established laws to specific cases is often deductive (with reservations).
  • Do NOT Confuse Deduction With General-to-Particular:
    • Deductive (Particular → General): 3, 5, and 7 are prime → all odd numbers between 2 and 8 are prime.
    • Inductive (General → Particular): All emeralds found were green → the next emerald will probably be green.
