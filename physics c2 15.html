Center of Mass

Translational Balance Point, 1D/2D/3D Coordinates, Regular Objects, and Center of Gravity Distinction

1 What is the Center of Mass?

Imagine balancing a long wooden ruler by placing a single finger underneath it at exactly the right point. That balance point is the center of mass.

Definition:

The center of mass (CM) is the unique point at which the entire mass of a body or system can be considered concentrated when analyzing its translational motion. It represents the balance point of an object's mass distribution.

The Hammer Analogy:

Because a hammer's head is much heavier than its wooden handle, its center of mass lies significantly closer to the heavy head, not at its geometric center. CM depends strictly on mass distribution, not geometric shape alone.

2 Center of Mass of Two Particles in 1D

Formula (Two Particles on \(x\)-axis):
$$\boxed{x_{\text{CM}} = \frac{m_1 x_1 + m_2 x_2}{m_1 + m_2}}$$

Heavier masses exert a greater pull, drawing the center of mass closer to themselves.

Example 1 — Two Masses on a Line

A \(2\text{ kg}\) mass is at \(x_1 = 0\text{ m}\) and a \(6\text{ kg}\) mass is at \(x_2 = 4\text{ m}\). Find \(x_{\text{CM}}\).

Step 1: \(m_1 = 2,\; x_1 = 0 \quad | \quad m_2 = 6,\; x_2 = 4\)
Step 2: \(x_{\text{CM}} = \frac{2(0) + 6(4)}{2 + 6} = \frac{24}{8}\)
Step 3: \(x_{\text{CM}} = \mathbf{3\text{ m}}\) (closer to the \(6\text{ kg}\) mass)
Example 2 — Masses on Opposite Sides of Origin

A \(4\text{ kg}\) mass is at \(x_1 = -3\text{ m}\) and a \(2\text{ kg}\) mass is at \(x_2 = +6\text{ m}\). Find \(x_{\text{CM}}\).

Step 1: \(x_{\text{CM}} = \frac{4(-3) + 2(6)}{4 + 2} = \frac{-12 + 12}{6}\)
Step 2: \(x_{\text{CM}} = \frac{0}{6} = \mathbf{0\text{ m}}\) (exactly at origin)

Exam Lesson: Negative positions must be preserved as negative values in calculations!

3 Center of Mass of Many Particles in 1D

General Formula (1D Multi-Particle System):
$$x_{\text{CM}} = \frac{\sum m_i x_i}{\sum m_i} = \frac{\sum m_i x_i}{M}$$

Where \(M = \sum m_i\) is the total mass of the system.

Example 3 — Three Particles Along the \(x\)-axis
  • Particle A: \(m = 2\text{ kg},\; x = 1\text{ m}\)
  • Particle B: \(m = 3\text{ kg},\; x = 4\text{ m}\)
  • Particle C: \(m = 5\text{ kg},\; x = 6\text{ m}\)
Step 1 (Total mass \(M\)): \(M = 2 + 3 + 5 = 10\text{ kg}\)
Step 2 (Moment sum): \(\sum m_i x_i = 2(1) + 3(4) + 5(6) = 2 + 12 + 30 = 44\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}\)
Step 3: \(x_{\text{CM}} = \frac{44}{10} = \mathbf{4.4\text{ m}}\)

4 Center of Mass in Two Dimensions (2D)

For particles distributed across a flat plane (\(xy\)-plane), compute each coordinate independently using total mass \(M\):

$$\boxed{x_{\text{CM}} = \frac{\sum m_i x_i}{M}}$$
$$\boxed{y_{\text{CM}} = \frac{\sum m_i y_i}{M}}$$
Example 4 — Three Particles in a Plane
  • Particle A: \(1\text{ kg}\) at \((0, 2)\text{ m}\)
  • Particle B: \(1\text{ kg}\) at \((2, 0)\text{ m}\)
  • Particle C: \(2\text{ kg}\) at \((2, 2)\text{ m}\)
Step 1 (Total mass \(M\)): \(M = 1 + 1 + 2 = 4\text{ kg}\)
Step 2 (\(x_{\text{CM}}\)): \(\frac{1(0) + 1(2) + 2(2)}{4} = \frac{6}{4} = \mathbf{1.5\text{ m}}\)
Step 3 (\(y_{\text{CM}}\)): \(\frac{1(2) + 1(0) + 2(2)}{4} = \frac{6}{4} = \mathbf{1.5\text{ m}} \implies \mathbf{(1.5,\; 1.5)\text{ m}}\)

5 Center of Mass in Three Dimensions (3D) & Vector Form

3D Coordinates & Position Vector:
\(x_{\text{CM}} = \frac{\sum m_i x_i}{M}, \quad y_{\text{CM}} = \frac{\sum m_i y_i}{M}, \quad z_{\text{CM}} = \frac{\sum m_i z_i}{M}\)
$$\vec{r}_{\text{CM}} = x_{\text{CM}}\hat{i} + y_{\text{CM}}\hat{j} + z_{\text{CM}}\hat{k} = \frac{\sum m_i \vec{r}_i}{M}$$
Example 5 — A Three-Dimensional System

Particle A: \(2\text{ kg}\) at \((1, 2, 3)\text{ m}\); Particle B: \(4\text{ kg}\) at \((4, 0, 1)\text{ m}\) (\(M = 6\text{ kg}\)):

\(x_{\text{CM}} = \frac{2(1) + 4(4)}{6} = \frac{18}{6} = 3\text{ m}\)
\(y_{\text{CM}} = \frac{2(2) + 4(0)}{6} = \frac{4}{6} = 0.67\text{ m}\)
\(z_{\text{CM}} = \frac{2(3) + 4(1)}{6} = \frac{10}{6} = 1.67\text{ m} \implies \mathbf{\vec{r}_{\text{CM}} = (3\hat{i} + 0.67\hat{j} + 1.67\hat{k})\text{ m}}\)

6 Worked Example — Four Objects Along the \(y\)-Axis

Example 6 — Four Objects on the \(y\)-Axis
  • \(2.00\text{ kg}\) at \(y = 3.00\text{ m}\)
  • \(3.00\text{ kg}\) at \(y = 2.50\text{ m}\)
  • \(2.50\text{ kg}\) at origin (\(y = 0\))
  • \(4.00\text{ kg}\) at \(y = -0.50\text{ m}\)
Step 1 (Total mass \(M\)): \(M = 2.00 + 3.00 + 2.50 + 4.00 = 11.5\text{ kg}\)
Step 2 (Moment sum): \(\sum m_i y_i = 2(3) + 3(2.5) + 2.5(0) + 4(-0.5) = 6 + 7.5 + 0 - 2 = 11.5\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}\)
Step 3: \(y_{\text{CM}} = \frac{11.5}{11.5} = \mathbf{+1.00\text{ m}}\) (\(1.00\text{ m}\) above origin)

7 Center of Mass of Regular Objects & CM vs. CG

Uniform Symmetrical Objects:
  • Rectangle: At geometric center.
  • Circular Disk: At center of circle.
  • Rod: At exact midpoint.
  • Sphere: At geometric center.
CM vs. Center of Gravity (CG):
  • CM: Represents mass distribution.
  • CG: Point through which resultant weight acts.

Near Earth's surface where \(g\) is uniform, CM and CG coincide.

8 Critical-Thinking Questions

  1. Two objects have equal masses, but one is placed farther from the origin. How does this affect the center of mass?
  2. Can the center of mass lie outside the material of an object? Think about a uniform ring.
  3. If you double every particle's mass without changing their positions, does the center of mass move?
  4. If a system's center of mass is at the origin, does that mean every particle must be at the origin?
  5. Why is the center of mass of a hammer closer to its heavy head than to the middle of its handle?
