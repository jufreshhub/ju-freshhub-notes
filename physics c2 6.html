Types of Forces

Contact & Non-Contact Forces, Static vs. Kinetic Friction, Normal Force, Tension, and Spring Force

1 The Two Main Categories of Forces

Imagine pushing a box across a floor, then imagine Earth pulling an apple downward. When you push the box, your hand touches it; when Earth pulls the apple, Earth does not need to touch it.

1. Contact Force

Requires direct physical contact between the interacting objects.

2. Non-Contact Force

Acts across empty space without direct physical contact between the objects.

2 Types of Contact Forces

A. Muscular Force

Muscles generate forces when we push, pull, lift, or move objects (e.g., lifting a bucket, pushing a table, pulling a door, or bodily movements). Muscular force is a contact force because it transmits through physical interaction with an object.

B. Frictional Force

Friction is a resisting force arising when two surfaces in contact slide—or tend to slide—relative to each other. It always acts parallel to the surfaces and opposite the actual sliding motion (kinetic) or tendency of motion (static).

1. Static Friction (\(f_s\))

Acts when surfaces are not sliding. Adjusts to match the applied force up to a maximum limit:

$$f_{s,\max} = \mu_s N$$
2. Kinetic Friction (\(f_k\))

Acts while one surface actively slides across another:

$$f_k = \mu_k N$$

Where \(\mu_s\) and \(\mu_k\) are coefficients of static and kinetic friction, and \(N\) is normal force. Usually \(\mu_k < \mu_s\), which is why starting a heavy box takes more force than keeping it sliding.

Worked Example 1 — Static Friction Adjustment

A student pushes a box with \(30\text{ N}\) horizontally. Maximum static friction is \(50\text{ N}\). Will it move?

Since \(30\text{ N} < 50\text{ N}\), static friction adjusts to \(30\text{ N} \implies F_{\text{net}} = 30 - 30 = 0\). Box remains at rest.

Exam Trap: Do not blindly set static friction equal to \(50\text{ N}\)—that is only its maximum threshold!

Worked Example 2 — Kinetic Friction & Acceleration

A \(10\text{ kg}\) box slides on a horizontal floor (\(\mu_k = 0.2\)) pushed by \(50\text{ N}\) forward (\(g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\)):

1. \(N = mg = 10(10) = 100\text{ N}\)  |  2. \(f_k = \mu_k N = 0.2(100) = 20\text{ N}\)
3. \(F_{\text{net}} = 50 - 20 = 30\text{ N} \implies 30 = 10a \implies \mathbf{a = 3\text{ m/s}^2\text{ forward}}\)

C. Normal Force (\(N\))

The normal force is the support force exerted by a surface on an object in contact with it, always acting perpendicular to the surface. On a flat horizontal table with no other vertical forces, \(N = mg\).

Worked Example 3 — Normal Force on an Inclined Plane

A \(5\text{ kg}\) block rests on a smooth plane inclined at \(\theta = 30^\circ\) (\(g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\)):

\(N = mg\cos\theta = 5(10)\cos 30^\circ = 50(0.866) = \mathbf{43.3\text{ N}}\)

Only the component of weight perpendicular to the incline is balanced by \(N\).

D. Applied Force & E. Tension Force (\(T\))

An applied force is exerted directly by a person or another object (e.g., pushing a table). Tension force is the pulling force transmitted through a stretched rope, string, cable, or wire.

Worked Example 4 — Tension in a Rope (\(m = 4\text{ kg}, g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\))
• Stationary: \(T - mg = 0 \implies T = 4(10) = \mathbf{40\text{ N}}\)
• Accelerating upward at \(a = 2\text{ m/s}^2\): \(T - mg = ma \implies T = m(g + a) = 4(10 + 2) = \mathbf{48\text{ N}}\)
F. Spring Force (Restoring)

A stretched or compressed ideal spring restores its equilibrium length via:

$$F = -kx$$

\(k =\) spring constant, \(x =\) displacement; minus sign indicates opposite direction.

G. Air Resistance

A resistive drag force opposing motion through air:

  • A parachute slowing a skydiver
  • A cyclist feeling air pushing back
  • Flat paper falling slower than a compact mass

3 Three Types of Non-Contact Forces

A. Gravitational Force

Attractive force between masses (e.g., Earth pulling objects down).

B. Magnetic Force

Exerted by magnets on magnetic materials or other magnets (attracts or repels).

C. Electrostatic Force

Force between electrically charged objects (opposite attract, like repel).

4 More Exam-Style Worked Examples

Example 5 — Will a Box Start Moving?

A box rests on a horizontal floor with \(f_{s,\max} = 35\text{ N}\). A student pushes with \(25\text{ N}\):

Since \(25\text{ N} < 35\text{ N}\), static friction balances it (\(f_s = 25\text{ N}\)) → \(F_{\text{net}} = 0\), box does not move.

Example 6 — Find the Coefficient of Kinetic Friction (\(\mu_k\))

A \(20\text{ kg}\) box slides at constant velocity under a \(40\text{ N}\) horizontal pull (\(g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\)):

Constant velocity \(\implies F_{\text{net}} = 0 \implies f_k = 40\text{ N}\). With \(N = 20(10) = 200\text{ N}\): \(\mu_k = \frac{40}{200} = \mathbf{0.20}\)

Example 7 — Tension During Downward Acceleration

A \(6\text{ kg}\) object hangs from a rope and accelerates downward at \(2\text{ m/s}^2\) (\(g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\)). Taking downward as positive:

\(mg - T = ma \implies 60 - T = 6(2) \implies 60 - T = 12 \implies \mathbf{T = 48\text{ N}}\)

5 Critical-Thinking Questions

Try reasoning these out conceptually before searching for formulas:

  1. If friction opposes motion, how can friction help you walk forward?
  2. A box is pushed with \(20\text{ N}\) but does not move. Is friction absent, or is another force balancing the push?
  3. Can an object experience a normal force without having a gravitational force acting on it?
  4. Why does a parachute increase air resistance?
