Metaphysics and Epistemology

Four Aspects of Reality, Skepticism vs. Agnosticism, Absolute vs. Relative Truth, and Five Sources of Knowledge

1 Metaphysics: The Ultimate Nature of Reality

Definition & Origin (Meta + Physika):

Metaphysics studies the ultimate nature of reality or existence (asking what lies beneath a glass of water—whether everything is physical matter, why anything exists, whether God exists, what happens after death, mind vs. body, and free will). From Greek meta (beyond/after) + physika (physics)—named by Aristotle's editor Andronicus of Rhodes for writings placed after Aristotle's treatise on physics.

A. Cosmological Aspect (Universe)

Studies the origin, nature, and development of the orderly universe—accident vs. design, purpose (e.g., stargazing and wondering how the universe began).

B. Theological Aspect (God)

Examines conceptions of God—existence, one vs. many, divine attributes, human relationship, and why suffering/evil exists if God is all-good and all-powerful.

C. Anthropological Aspect (Human Beings)

Mind-body relationship, soul, whether people are born good/evil/neutral, and free will vs. determinism (environment, heredity, or divine control).

D. Ontological Aspect (Existence Itself)

What it means to exist—matter vs. spirit, one/two/many elements, fixed stability vs. constant change, and whether reality is naturally orderly.

Memory Clue: Cosmology (Universe) → Theology (God) → Anthropology (Human) → Ontology (Existence)

2 Epistemology: Theory & Debates of Knowledge

From Greek episteme (knowledge) + logos (study of), epistemology studies the nature, scope, meaning, and reliability of knowledge (like asking how you know it is raining—via hearing roof drops, seeing rain, or trusting a weather report—and how knowledge differs from belief or opinion).

A. Skepticism (vs. Gorgias)

Claims people cannot acquire reliable knowledge and searching for truth is futile (Sophist Gorgias: nothing exists; if it did, we couldn't know it). Complete skepticism makes intelligent action impossible.

B. Agnosticism

A specific profession of ignorance concerning the existence or nonexistence of God (distinct from general skepticism about all knowledge).

3 Five Sources of Human Knowledge (E-R-I-R-A)

1. Empiricism (Knowledge Through the Senses)

Acquiring knowledge via sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch (knowing fire is hot). Advantage: Repeatable and publicly testable. Limitations: Fatigue, illness, undetectable sound/light waves, and illusions like the bent-stick illusion in water.

2. Rationalism (Reason & Logic)

Concluding Socrates is mortal from all humans are mortal without seeing him die. Includes less extreme vs. extreme rationalism. Limit: Consistent logic still depends on valid premises.

3. Intuition (Direct Insight)

Direct apprehension without conscious reasoning (sudden “eureka!” idea later tested scientifically). Limit: Unreliable unless cross-checked against other methods.

4. Revelation (Supernatural)

Communication from transcendent divine reality (important in religion). Limit: Accepted by faith; cannot be empirically proved/disproved; human interpretation may distort.

5. Authority (Experts & Tradition)

Accepted from trusted experts or tradition (anatomy textbooks, physics teachers, reference books)—common in classrooms yet still requires critical scrutiny.

METAPHYSICS = REALITY Looking at a tree: What is the ultimate nature and existence of this tree?
EPISTEMOLOGY = KNOWLEDGE Looking at a tree: How do I know it exists, and can I trust my senses?
