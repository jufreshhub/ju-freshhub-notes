Techniques of Recognizing Arguments

Factual & Inferential Claims, Five Non-Argumentative Passages, Explanations vs. Arguments, and Necessary/Sufficient Conditions

1 Recognizing Argumentative Passages

Compare Passage A (“Students should attend classes regularly because regular attendance improves their understanding”) with Passage B (“Students attend classes in the morning. Some study in the library...”). A passage contains an argument only if it purports to prove something—meaning two essential conditions are fulfilled (regardless of whether the premises actually turn out true or strong):

Condition 1: Factual Claim

At least one statement claims to present evidence or reasons (e.g., “Jimma University has many students because it has several academic departments”).

Condition 2: Inferential Claim

A claim that the evidence supports or implies a conclusion—expressed either explicitly (“Gamachuu is my biological father because my mother told me so”) or implicitly without indicator words (“Genetic modification of food is risky...”).

Watch Out for Indicator Words (The “Since” Trap):
• Not an Argument (Temporal “since”): “Since Edison invented the phonograph, many technological developments have occurred.” (Means from the time that.)
• Argument (Inferential “since”): “Since Edison invented the phonograph, he deserves credit for a major technological development.” (Introduces a reason.)

2 Five Major Non-Argumentative Passages

A. Five Simple Non-Inferential Passages
  • 1. Warnings: Protect from harm (“Never share your password with strangers”).
  • 2. Pieces of advice: Recommend action (“After class, I suggest that you review your notes”).
  • 3. Belief or opinion: Express conviction without proof (“We believe our university should produce highly skilled graduates”).
  • 4. Loosely associated statements: Same topic without logical link (“Jimma is a university town. Ethiopia has many regions. Students enjoy sports”).
  • 5. Reports: Communicate facts/events (“The university opened a new library. Hundreds visited during the first week”).
B. Expository Passages

Develop a topic sentence (“Football is popular... two teams...” = non-argument), unless details prove the claim (“Human skin protects from microbes... oil glands kill bacteria...” = argument).

C. Illustrations

Show examples (“Thus, oxygen is O2, water is H2O, NaCl...” = non-argument), unless used as proof (“Not all cancers are life-threatening. For example, basal cell carcinoma...” = argument from example).

D. Explanations (Explanandum vs. Explanans)

Clarifies why an already-accepted fact occurs: in “Cows digest grass because their digestive systems contain special enzymes,” Explanandum = accepted event (cows digest grass); Explanans = causal statement (enzymes not found in humans). Notice identical wording can act as an argument if the reader doubts the fact!

ArgumentExplanation
Tries to prove an unestablished claimTries to clarify why an accepted fact happens
Moves from evidence → conclusionMoves from accepted fact → its cause
E. Conditional Statements (If… then…)

Consists of an Antecedent (if-clause: you study hard) and a Consequent (then-clause: you will score an A). Three key rules: (1) A single conditional statement is NOT an argument (asserts neither truth individually); (2) Conditionals can serve as a premise or conclusion in an argument (If selling secrets → traitor; He is selling secrets → Traitor); (3) Its inferential link can be rewritten as an argument.

3 Necessary vs. Sufficient Conditions & Quick Matrix

Sufficient Condition (“Enough to Guarantee”)

In “If something is a dog, then it is an animal,” being a dog is sufficient for being an animal—knowing it's a dog guarantees it's an animal.

Necessary Condition (“Must Be Present”)

Being an animal is necessary for being a dog (a dog cannot exist without being an animal), though an animal could be a cat or mouse.
