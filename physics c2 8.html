Uniform Circular Motion

Centripetal Acceleration, Orbital Period, Centripetal vs. Centrifugal Forces, and Limiting Cornering Speed

Can an object move at constant speed and still accelerate?

Yes! Imagine a car travelling around a circular track at a constant speed of \(20\text{ m/s}\). Its speed stays fixed, yet its direction changes continuously. Because velocity includes both speed and direction, its velocity changes—and therefore the car accelerates.

1 What is Uniform Circular Motion?

Definition: Uniform circular motion is the motion of an object along a circular path at constant speed. (Uniform = constant speed; Circular = circular path.)

  • A car moving around a circular track at constant speed.
  • A rotating washing machine drum.
  • A stone tied to a string and whirled in a circle.

2 Centripetal Acceleration (\(a_c\))

At every instant, velocity points along the tangent to the circle, perpendicular to the radius, while centripetal (center-seeking) acceleration points along the radius toward the center:

$$\boxed{a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}}$$

Where \(a_c\) = centripetal acceleration (\(\text{m/s}^2\)), \(v\) = constant speed (\(\text{m/s}\)), and \(r\) = radius (\(\text{m}\)).

Scaling Relationships (\(a_c \propto v^2\) and \(a_c \propto \frac{1}{r}\)):
  • Double speed → acceleration becomes 4× greater.
  • Triple speed → acceleration becomes 9× greater.
  • Double radius (at constant speed) → acceleration becomes half.
Worked Example 1 — Basic Centripetal Acceleration

A car moves around a circular track of radius \(10\text{ m}\) at a constant speed of \(5\text{ m/s}\). Find its centripetal acceleration.

Step 1: List given values and formula
Given: \(v = 5\text{ m/s}, \quad r = 10\text{ m}\)  |  Formula: \(a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}\)
Step 2: Substitute and evaluate
\(a_c = \frac{5^2}{10} = \frac{25}{10} = \mathbf{2.5\text{ m/s}^2}\) (toward center)
Doubling Check (if \(v\) increases to \(10\text{ m/s}\)):
\(a_c = \frac{10^2}{10} = \frac{100}{10} = 10\text{ m/s}^2\) (exact 4× increase!)

3 Period of Circular Motion (\(T\))

The period, represented by \(T\), is the time required for one complete revolution. Because one revolution covers the circumference \(C = 2\pi r\):

$$\boxed{v = \frac{2\pi r}{T}}$$
$$\boxed{T = \frac{2\pi r}{v}}$$
$$\boxed{a_c = \frac{4\pi^2 r}{T^2}}$$
Worked Example 2 — Finding Speed From Period

A wheel rotates in a circular path of radius \(r = 2\text{ m}\) completing one revolution in \(T = 4\text{ s}\).

\(v = \frac{2\pi r}{T} = \frac{2\pi(2)}{4} = \pi \approx \mathbf{3.14\text{ m/s}}\)
Worked Example 3 — Finding Acceleration Using Period

Using the same wheel (\(r = 2\text{ m},\; v \approx 3.14\text{ m/s},\; T = 4\text{ s}\)):

Method 1: \(a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{3.14^2}{2} \approx \mathbf{4.93\text{ m/s}^2}\)
Method 2 (Direct): \(a_c = \frac{4\pi^2 r}{T^2} = \frac{4\pi^2(2)}{4^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{2} \approx \mathbf{4.93\text{ m/s}^2}\)

4 Centripetal Force (\(F_c\))

Connecting Newton's Second Law (\(F_{\text{net}} = ma\)) with \(a_c = \frac{v^2}{r}\):

$$\boxed{F_c = ma_c = \frac{mv^2}{r}}$$

Very Important: Centripetal force is NOT a new type of force—it is simply the net inward force keeping an object on a circular path.

Depending on the situation, centripetal force may be provided by tension in a string, gravitational force, friction between tyres and road, or normal force from a surface:

Worked Example 4 — Centripetal Force on a Rotating Stone

A stone of mass \(2\text{ kg}\) rotates in a circle of radius \(0.5\text{ m}\) at \(4\text{ m/s}\). Find the centripetal force.

\(F_c = \frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{2(4^2)}{0.5} = \frac{32}{0.5} = \mathbf{64\text{ N inward}}\)
Worked Example 5 — What Happens If the String Breaks?

Answer: It moves in a straight line tangent to the circular path at the breaking point (along its instantaneous velocity).

Exam Trap: The stone does NOT fly radially outward from the center—it travels tangentially!

5 Centripetal Force vs. Centrifugal Force

When a car turns left, passengers feel pushed toward the right side because their bodies resist turning due to inertia.

Centripetal ForceCentrifugal Force
Directed toward the centerAppears directed away from the center
Required for circular motionApparent force in a rotating frame
Real net inward forceNot a real interaction in an inertial frame

Rule: Never draw an outward centrifugal force in an ordinary inertial-frame free-body diagram.

6 Advanced Applied Circular Motion Examples

Example 6 — Athlete Rotating a Discus

An athlete swings a discus along a circle of radius \(r = 1.06\text{ m}\) at maximum speed \(v = 20\text{ m/s}\). Find the maximum centripetal acceleration.

\(a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \frac{20^2}{1.06} = \frac{400}{1.06} \approx \mathbf{377.36\text{ m/s}^2}\)
Example 7 — Car Turning on a Flat Road

A \(1000\text{ kg}\) car rounds a flat circular road of radius \(50\text{ m}\) at \(10\text{ m/s}\). Find the minimum static friction force required.

Step 1: On a flat road, static friction provides the centripetal force.
Step 2: \(F_c = \frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{1000(10^2)}{50} = \frac{100000}{50} = \mathbf{2000\text{ N}}\)
Example 8 — Finding Maximum Speed Before Slipping

A car travels on a flat curve of radius \(r = 40\text{ m}\) where \(\mu_s = 0.5\) (\(g = 10\text{ m/s}^2\)). Find the maximum safe speed without slipping.

Step 1: Express maximum static friction
\(f_{s,\max} = \mu_s N = \mu_s mg\)
Step 2: Equate limiting friction to required centripetal force
\(\frac{mv^2}{r} = \mu_s mg\)
Cancel mass \(m\) from both sides: \(\frac{v^2}{r} = \mu_s g \implies v = \sqrt{\mu_s g r}\)
Step 3: Substitute numerical values
\(v = \sqrt{0.5(10)(40)} = \sqrt{200} \approx \mathbf{14.14\text{ m/s}}\)

Critical Observation: Mass cancels completely! Maximum flat cornering speed does not depend on the car's mass.

8 Critical-Thinking Questions

  1. If a car moves around a circle at constant speed, why is its acceleration not zero?
  2. If the speed of a rotating object doubles, why does the centripetal force become four times greater?
  3. Why does a car need friction to turn on a flat road?
  4. If a string breaks while a stone is rotating, why does the stone move tangentially rather than radially outward?
  5. Why can two objects with different masses have the same centripetal acceleration when moving at the same speed along circles of equal radius?
