Natural Numbers

Counting Sets, Algebraic Axioms, Divisibility, GCF, and LCM

1 Foundations of Natural Numbers

The Counting Analogy: Imagine counting students walking through the gates of Jimma University. You start with the first student as \(1\), the next as \(2\), followed by \(3\), \(4\), and so on. You never start counting physical objects with negative numbers or fractions. These instinctive counting values form the natural numbers.
Mathematical Definition: Natural numbers are positive integers used for counting discrete entities and ordering items. They are symbolized by the set \(\mathbb{N}\): $$\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, \dots\}$$

Core Principles to Memorize:

  • Smallest Element: The smallest natural number is \(1\).
  • Unbounded Set: There is no largest natural number; the set \(\mathbb{N}\) is infinitely ascending.
  • Zero Exclusion: Under this university standard, \(0\) is strictly not a natural number (\(0 \notin \mathbb{N}\)). Zero belongs to the set of whole numbers \(\mathbb{W} = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}\).
Examination Warning: If an assessment asks "Which of the following is NOT a natural number?" with options containing positive integers and \(0\), the correct answer is always \(0\).

2 Binary Operations: Addition & Multiplication

Two fundamental binary operations are intrinsically defined over the set of natural numbers:

A. Addition \((+)\)

Addition signifies the aggregation or combination of separate quantities into a single collection.

$$a + b = c$$

Where \(a\) and \(b\) are the terms (addends), and \(c\) is their sum.

B. Multiplication \((\times)\)

Multiplication represents repeated addition of uniform groups. For example, \(3\) bags of \(4\) books gives \(4 + 4 + 4 = 3 \times 4 = 12\).

$$a \times b = c$$

Where \(a\) and \(b\) are the factors, and \(c\) is the product.

Operation Input Elements Result Name
Addition \((+)\) Terms (or Addends) Sum
Multiplication \((\times)\) Factors (or Multiplicands) Product

3 Fundamental Algebraic Properties

1. Closure Property Stay in the set

A set is closed under an operation if applying the operation to any two elements always produces an element inside the exact same set.

  • Addition: \(\forall a, b \in \mathbb{N} \implies a + b \in \mathbb{N}\). (e.g., \(4 + 5 = 9 \in \mathbb{N}\)).
  • Multiplication: \(\forall a, b \in \mathbb{N} \implies a \times b \in \mathbb{N}\). (e.g., \(4 \times 5 = 20 \in \mathbb{N}\)).
Counterexamples for Subtraction & Division:
  • \(3 - 7 = -4 \notin \mathbb{N}\) (Not closed under subtraction).
  • \(3 \div 2 = 1.5 \notin \mathbb{N}\) (Not closed under division).

2. Commutative Property Change order

Permuting (switching) the order of operands does not alter the resulting value:

\(a + b = b + a\)
\(3 + 5 = 5 + 3 = 8\)
\(a \times b = b \times a\)
\(3 \times 5 = 5 \times 3 = 15\)

Note: Subtraction is not commutative because \(8 - 3 \neq 3 - 8\).

3. Associative Property Change grouping

Regrouping terms or factors using parentheses does not affect the ultimate computation:

$$(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$$

\((3 + 4) + 5 = 7 + 5 = 12 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad 3 + (4 + 5) = 3 + 9 = 12\)

$$(a \times b) \times c = a \times (b \times c)$$

\((2 \times 4) \times 5 = 8 \times 5 = 40 \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad 2 \times (4 \times 5) = 2 \times 20 = 40\)

4. Identity Element Leave unchanged

An identity element operates on any number \(a\) without modifying its identity:

$$a \times 1 = 1 \times a = a$$

Thus, the number \(1\) is the multiplicative identity in \(\mathbb{N}\).

Crucial Identity Distinction: The additive identity is \(0\) (since \(a + 0 = a\)). However, because \(0 \notin \mathbb{N}\), the set of natural numbers possesses no additive identity. It only possesses a multiplicative identity (\(1\)).

5. Distributive Property Multiply through brackets

Multiplication distributes linearly across terms enclosed within an addition bracket:

$$a(b + c) = ab + ac$$

Example: \(3 \times (5 + 7) = 3 \times 12 = 36\). Expanding directly: \((3 \times 5) + (3 \times 7) = 15 + 21 = 36\). Both evaluations yield identical results.

4 Factors and Divisibility

Suppose you wish to partition \(12\) students into identical, equal-sized study groups without leaving anyone unassigned. The permissible group sizes correspond precisely to the factors of \(12\).

Definition: If \(a, b, c \in \mathbb{N}\) such that \(a \times b = c\), then \(a\) and \(b\) are called factors (or exact divisors) of \(c\). A factor divides the target number leaving zero remainder.

Example: Factor Set of 15

\(1 \times 15 = 15\)  |  \(3 \times 5 = 15\)

The complete factor set is: \(F_{15} = \{1, 3, 5, 15\}\).

5 Classification of Natural Numbers

Natural numbers are categorized systematically based on parity and divisor counts:

Even Numbers

Natural numbers divisible by \(2\) with remainder \(0\).

\(\{2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, \dots\}\)

Odd Numbers

Natural numbers not divisible by \(2\) (remainder \(1\)).

\(\{1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, \dots\}\)

Prime Numbers

Numbers possessing exactly two distinct factors: \(1\) and itself.

\(\{2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, \dots\}\)

Composite Numbers

Numbers possessing three or more distinct factors.

\(\{4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, \dots\}\)

Vital Examination Truths:
  • The Number \(1\) is Neither Prime nor Composite: A prime number requires exactly two distinct factors; a composite requires three or more. The number \(1\) has only one factor (\(1\)); thus it qualifies as neither.
  • The Number \(2\) is the Only Even Prime: Every other even number is divisible by \(2\) in addition to \(1\) and itself, rendering all higher even numbers composite.

6 Prime Factorization

Prime factorization is the analytical procedure of decomposing a composite integer entirely into a product of its constituent prime factors.

The Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (Unique Factorization Theorem): Every composite integer greater than \(1\) can be represented uniquely as a product of prime numbers, up to the rearrangement of the factors.
Factorization of 12:

\(12 = 2 \times 6 = 2 \times (2 \times 3) = 2^2 \times 3\)

Factorization of 30:

\(30 = 2 \times 3 \times 5\)

Factorization of 180:

\(180 = 18 \times 10 = (2 \times 3^2) \times (2 \times 5) = 2^2 \times 3^2 \times 5\)

7 Greatest Common Factor (GCF / GCD)

The Greatest Common Factor (or Greatest Common Divisor, \(\gcd\)) of two or more natural numbers is the largest natural number that divides each of them without remainder.

Method 1: Factor Set Intersection for \(\text{GCF}(24, 60)\)

\(F_{24} = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, \mathbf{12}, 24\}\)

\(F_{60} = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, \mathbf{12}, 15, 20, 30, 60\}\)

Intersection: \(\{1, 2, 3, 4, 6, \mathbf{12}\} \implies \text{GCF}(24, 60) = 12\).

Method 2: Prime Factorization (Standard Exam Algorithm)

\(24 = 2^3 \times 3^1\)

\(60 = 2^2 \times 3^1 \times 5^1\)

Rule: Take only common prime factors raised to their lowest powers:
$$\text{GCF}(24, 60) = 2^{\min(3, 2)} \times 3^{\min(1, 1)} = 2^2 \times 3^1 = 12$$
Relatively Prime (Coprime) Numbers:

Two numbers \(a\) and \(b\) are defined as relatively prime if their greatest common factor is strictly \(1\): $$\text{GCF}(a, b) = 1$$ Example: \(8\) and \(15\) share no common factors other than \(1\) (\(8 = 2^3\), \(15 = 3 \times 5\)), so \(\text{GCF}(8, 15) = 1\).

8 Least Common Multiple (LCM)

The Least Common Multiple of two natural numbers is the smallest natural number divisible by both.

The Runner Analogy: Two students run on an athletic track. Student A completes a lap every \(6\) minutes; Student B completes a lap every \(8\) minutes. They will cross the start line simultaneously at the smallest shared multiple: $$\text{Multiples of } 6: \{6, 12, 18, \mathbf{24}, 30, \dots\}$$ $$\text{Multiples of } 8: \{8, 16, \mathbf{24}, 32, \dots\} \implies \text{LCM}(6, 8) = 24$$
Prime Factorization Algorithm for LCM:

\(6 = 2^1 \times 3^1\)

\(8 = 2^3\)

Rule: Take every prime factor present raised to its highest power:
$$\text{LCM}(6, 8) = 2^{\max(1, 3)} \times 3^1 = 2^3 \times 3 = 8 \times 3 = 24$$
