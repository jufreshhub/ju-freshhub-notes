Displacement, Velocity and Acceleration in 1D and 2D

Reference Frames, Average vs. Instantaneous Rates, Speed vs. Velocity, and Conceptual Motion Traps

1 First, What is Kinematics?

Imagine watching a car moving along a road. You can describe where the car is, how far it travels, how fast it moves, and whether its velocity changes. You can do all of this without knowing whether the engine is powerful or what force pushes the car. That is kinematics.

Definition: Kinematics is the branch of mechanics that describes the motion of objects without considering the causes of motion, such as forces.
Kinematics Dynamics
Describes motion Studies motion and its causes
How does an object move? Why does it move?
Displacement, velocity, acceleration Force, mass, and their effects
Example: A Ball Falls From a Building
• Kinematics asks: How fast is it falling? How far has it fallen?
• Dynamics asks: What causes it to fall?

Remember: Kinematics describes motion; dynamics explains motion through its causes.

2 Position — Where is the Object?

Imagine you are standing beside a straight road. You choose a tree as your reference point and call its position zero. A car \(10\text{ metres}\) to the right of the tree has position \(x = +10\text{ m}\), while another car \(5\text{ metres}\) to the left has position \(x = -5\text{ m}\).

Figure 1: Position Relative to a Chosen Reference Frame
Definition:

Position is the location of an object with respect to a chosen reference point. It depends entirely on the reference point and coordinate system you select.

Think critically: If you describe a person's position simply as “10 metres”, that information is incomplete! Ten metres from where, and in which direction? That is why a reference point matters.

3 Displacement — How Much Did Position Change?

Imagine walking from your classroom to the library. Your starting position is \(x_i = 2\text{ m}\), and your final position is \(x_f = 12\text{ m}\) along the positive \(x\)-axis:

One Dimension (1D)
$$\boxed{\Delta x = x_f - x_i}$$

\(x_f = \text{final position}\), \(x_i = \text{initial position}\).
\(\Delta x = 12 - 2 = \mathbf{+10\text{ m}}\) (positive direction).

Two Dimensions (2D)
$$\boxed{\Delta\vec{r} = \vec{r}_f - \vec{r}_i}$$

Walking \(3\text{ m east}\) and \(4\text{ m north}\):
Distance = \(7\text{ m}\); Displacement = \(\sqrt{3^2+4^2} = \mathbf{5\text{ m}}\).

Figure 2: Distance Travelled vs. Vector Displacement
The Key Distinction:
• Distance: Total length of the actual path travelled (scalar).
• Displacement: Direct straight-line change from initial position to final position (vector).
Critical-Thinking Question 1

A student walks \(8\text{ m east}\), then \(8\text{ m west}\), returning to the starting point.

  • What is the total distance?
  • What is the displacement?
  • Can an object travel a nonzero distance but have zero displacement?

Think carefully about the difference before continuing.

4 Average Velocity vs. Average Speed

Average Velocity (Vector)
$$v_{\text{av}} = \frac{x_f - x_i}{t_f - t_i} \quad \text{and} \quad \vec{v}_{\text{av}} = \frac{\Delta\vec{r}}{\Delta t}$$

Example: Moving \(20\text{ m east}\) in \(5\text{ s} \implies v_{\text{av}} = \frac{20}{5} = \mathbf{4\text{ m/s east}}\). Depends on displacement!

Average Speed (Scalar)
$$\text{Average Speed} = \frac{\text{Total Distance}}{\text{Total Time}}$$

Has magnitude only (no direction). Depends on the total path length travelled.

Classic Worked Example: Round Trip (Equal Distances, Different Speeds)

A person walks from point \(A\) to point \(B\) at \(5\text{ m/s}\), then returns from \(B\) to \(A\) at \(3\text{ m/s}\). The two sections have equal distance \(d\) (assume each leg is \(15\text{ m}\)).

Time going: \(t_1 = \frac{15}{5} = 3\text{ s}\)  |  Time returning: \(t_2 = \frac{15}{3} = 5\text{ s}\)
Total distance: \(S = 15 + 15 = 30\text{ m}\)  |  Total time: \(t = 3 + 5 = 8\text{ s}\)
Average Speed: \(v_{\text{speed}} = \frac{30}{8} = \mathbf{3.75\text{ m/s}}\)
Average Velocity: Since \(\Delta x = 0 \implies \mathbf{v_{\text{av}} = 0\text{ m/s}}\)

Important Exam Concept: Here, average speed is non-zero (\(3.75\text{ m/s}\)), while average velocity is zero!

Critical-Thinking Question 2

In that example, why is the average speed \(3.75\text{ m/s}\) instead of the ordinary average of the two speeds (\(\frac{5 + 3}{2} = 4\text{ m/s}\))?

When would taking the simple average of two speeds give the wrong answer? Try to discover the reason yourself.

5 Instantaneous Velocity & Acceleration

A. Instantaneous Velocity — How Fast Right Now?

Imagine a car travelling along a highway whose speedometer displays \(80\text{ km/h}\) at a single moment—that is its instantaneous speed. Instantaneous velocity is the velocity at a specific instant in time, defined as the limit of average velocity as \(\Delta t\) approaches zero:

$$\boxed{\vec{v} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta\vec{r}}{\Delta t}}$$

You don't need to be frightened by the limit symbol! Think of it simply:

  • Average velocity: How fast did I move over the entire time interval?
  • Instantaneous velocity: How fast am I moving at this exact moment? (Its magnitude is instantaneous speed.)

B. Average & Instantaneous Acceleration

If Car A moves at a constant \(20\text{ m/s}\) while Car B increases from \(20\text{ m/s}\) to \(30\text{ m/s}\), Car B is accelerating because its velocity changes with time. The SI unit of acceleration is \(\text{m/s}^2\).

Average Acceleration
$$\vec{a}_{\text{av}} = \frac{\Delta\vec{v}}{\Delta t} = \frac{\vec{v}_f - \vec{v}_i}{t_f - t_i}$$
Instantaneous Acceleration
$$\vec{a} = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \frac{\Delta\vec{v}}{\Delta t}$$
Worked Example:

A car increases its velocity from \(v_i = 10\text{ m/s}\) to \(v_f = 30\text{ m/s}\) in \(\Delta t = 5\text{ s}\):

\(a = \frac{30 - 10}{5} = \mathbf{4\text{ m/s}^2}\)   (Velocity increases by \(4\text{ m/s}\) every second.)

6 Acceleration Does Not Always Mean Speeding Up!

One of the biggest conceptual traps in mechanics is assuming acceleration only means getting faster:

Car Moves East & Slows Down

• Velocity points East
• Acceleration points West

Opposite directions → Speed decreases

Car Moves West & Speeds Up

• Velocity points West
• Acceleration points West

Same direction → Speed increases
