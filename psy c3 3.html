Behavioral Theories of Learning

Classical Conditioning (Pavlov), Operant Conditioning (Skinner), Reinforcement Schedules, Punishment, and Shaping

1 Stimulus-Response Framework

Whether hearing a song that triggers a painful memory, studying harder for teacher praise, or stopping touching a hot pot after a burn, behavioral theories explain observable learning through stimulus–response associations: a stimulus (heat from a pot) produces a response (withdrawing your hand).

Classical Conditioning (Ivan Pavlov)

Learning through association between stimuli; typically involves involuntary responses.

Operant Conditioning (B. F. Skinner)

Learning through consequences of voluntary behavior (reinforcement & punishment).

2 Classical Conditioning (Ivan Pavlov's Experiment)

Russian physiologist Ivan Pavlov discovered classical conditioning: a previously neutral stimulus (bell) begins producing a learned response (salivation) after repeated pairing with a natural stimulus (food).

Stage 1: Before Conditioning

Bell → No salivation.
Food (UCS) → Salivation (UCR).

Stage 2: During Conditioning

Bell + Food paired repeatedly → Salivation.

Stage 3: After Conditioning

Bell alone (CS) → Salivation (CR)!

3 Four Principles of Classical Conditioning

A. Stimulus Generalization

Responding to stimuli similar to the CS (salivating to a doorbell or telephone bell as well as a dinner bell).

B. Stimulus Discrimination

Distinguishing between similar stimuli and responding only to the exact CS (dinner bell only, not telephone bell).

C. Extinction

CR gradually weakens and stops when CS is presented repeatedly without UCS (inhibits CR rather than erasing it completely).

D. Spontaneous Recovery

Reappearance of an extinguished CR after a rest period without further training.

4 Operant Conditioning (B. F. Skinner) & Reinforcers

Associated with B. F. Skinner, operant conditioning modifies voluntary behavior through its consequences (e.g., studying hard → scoring 95% & earning praise → studying hard again). Skinner identified three consequences: Neutral consequence (no effect on likelihood), Reinforcement (strengthens behavior), and Punishment (weakens behavior).

Primary vs. Secondary Reinforcers

1. Primary Reinforcers (Biological Needs)

Naturally satisfy biological needs without any prior learning: food, water, light, stroking of the skin, and comfortable air temperature.

2. Secondary Reinforcers (Learned Value)

Become rewarding through association with primary or other learned rewards: money, praise, applause, good grades, awards, certificates, and gold stars.

5 Reinforcement vs. Punishment & Escape vs. Avoidance

Crucial Rule: Positive = Add something; Negative = Remove something (NOT good vs. bad!). Reinforcement always increases behavior; Punishment always decreases behavior.
TypeActionEffectExample
Positive ReinforcementAdd desirableIncreasesTeacher praises correct answer
Negative ReinforcementRemove unpleasantIncreasesNagging stops once you start studying
Positive PunishmentAdd unpleasantDecreasesExtra work assigned for breaking rule
Negative PunishmentRemove desirableDecreasesPhone privileges removed for misuse
Escape Learning:

Stopping an unpleasant stimulus already occurring (leaving a noisy room to escape noise).

Avoidance Learning:

Preventing an unpleasant stimulus before it happens (leaving early to avoid heavy traffic).

Effectiveness & Limitations of Punishment:

Punishment works best with immediacy, consistency, and appropriate intensity. However, it can fail by triggering fear/anger that spreads to the teacher or classroom (e.g., shouting at students asking questions), acting only temporarily when the punisher is absent, failing to teach replacement behavior, or accidentally reinforcing attention-seeking.

6 Schedules of Reinforcement, Shaping & Classroom Applications

Continuous reinforcement reinforces every correct response (fast initial learning); intermittent (partial) reinforcement reinforces occasionally, creating stronger resistance to extinction via four schedules (Ratio = responses; Interval = time):

Intermittent ScheduleRuleExample
Fixed-Ratio (FR)Fixed number of responsesBonus after every 10 products
Variable-Ratio (VR)Unpredictable response count (highest rate/resistance)Slot machine payouts
Fixed-Interval (FI)Fixed elapsed timeMonthly allowance every 30 days
Variable-Interval (VI)Varying elapsed timeSurprise quizzes
Shaping (Successive Approximations)

Teaching complex behavior by rewarding small progressive steps (successive approximations)—such as rewarding a child first for holding a fork, then both utensils, then picking up food, until eating properly.

Seven Educational Applications of Operant Conditioning:

(1) Conditioning study behavior via prizes/praise/marks; (2) Conditioning positive classroom attitudes; (3) Managing problem behavior with encouragement; (4) Reducing anxieties via gradual desensitization; (5) Conditioning group discussion participation; (6) Stepped cognitive lessons with immediate feedback; (7) Shaping complex skills step by step.
