Geological Time Scale and Age Dating Techniques

Eons, Eras, Periods & Epochs, Major Life Evolutions, Relative vs. Absolute Dating, Carbon-14 & Potassium-Argon

1 The Geological Time Scale & Hierarchy

The geological time scale organizes Earth's immense history—like chapters in a very long book—describing major geological events, climate shifts, and biological evolution. Earth formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago, and earliest life originated around 3.5 billion years ago (the time prior to life is included in the Precambrian).

1. EON Largest unit
2. ERA Subdivision of Eon
3. PERIOD Subdivision of Era
4. EPOCH Smallest listed unit

Memory Order (Largest to Smallest): Eon → Era → Period → Epoch. Divisions are based on major events like mass extinctions rather than equal lengths.

2 The Four Major Geological Eras (P → P → M → C)

A. Precambrian Era 4,500–600 Million Years Ago

Oldest and longest division, covering approximately five-sixths (5/6) of Earth's history. Earth formed (~4.5B yrs ago), earliest rocks (~4B yrs ago), earliest fossils (~3.5B yrs ago), and marine invertebrates (~600M yrs ago).

3 Age Dating Techniques (Relative & Absolute)

A. Relative Dating (Older vs. Younger Comparison)

Determines relative order rather than exact numerical years via Fossil Evidence and Position of Rock Layers (“Younger rocks generally lie on top of older rocks,” just like a sandwich).

Worked Example 1: A cliff has undisturbed layers A (bottom), B (middle), and C (top). Oldest = Layer A; Youngest = Layer C.
B. Absolute (Radiometric) Dating & Half-Life

Estimates numerical age using radioactive clocks (e.g., Uranium, Thorium). Half-life is the time needed for half of an original radioactive sample to decay:

$$100\text{ g} \xrightarrow{\text{1 half-life}} 50\text{ g} \xrightarrow{\text{2 half-lives}} 25\text{ g} \xrightarrow{\text{3 half-lives}} 12.5\text{ g}$$
Worked Example 2:
Half-life = \(10\text{ yrs}\), Original mass = \(80\text{ g}\). Remaining after \(20\text{ yrs}\)?
Step 1: Half-lives = \(20 \div 10 = 2\)
Step 2: \(80 \to 40 \to \mathbf{20\text{ g}}\)
Carbon-14 Technique (Organisms):

Dates remains of once-living organisms (half-life = \(5,730\text{ years}\)). Not used for general ancient rocks.

Example 3: \(100\text{ g}\) C-14 after \(11,460\text{ yrs}\):
\(11,460 \div 5,730 = 2 \implies 100 \to 50 \to \mathbf{25\text{ g}}\)
Potassium-Argon Technique (Rocks):

Widely used for dating rocks containing Potassium-40 (Micas, Feldspars, Hornblendes).

Limitation: Heating above \(125^\circ\text{C}\) leaks argon gas, resetting the clock to the last heating event.
