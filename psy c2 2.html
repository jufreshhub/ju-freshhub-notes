Sensory Laws: Thresholds and Adaptation

Absolute Threshold, Response Bias, Difference Threshold (JND), Weber's Law, and Sensory Adaptation

Imagine sitting in a quiet room: initially you hear the clock ticking clearly, yet minutes later you barely notice it. Two fundamental sensory laws govern how much stimulation our senses require to register signals, detect changes, and filter out constant background stimuli:

1. Sensory Threshold

The minimum level of stimulation intensity needed for a sensation or change to be detected.

2. Sensory Adaptation

The decreasing responsiveness of sensory receptors to a continuous, unchanging stimulus.

1 Absolute Threshold (Limen) & Response Bias

Consider adding sugar to coffee: at first no sweetness registers, then a small pinch still goes unnoticed, until enough sugar reaches the sensory threshold. Specifically, the absolute threshold (limen) is the minimum amount of stimulation a person can detect—the weakest stimulus noticeable (such as a very faint sound heard only on the third, slightly louder attempt).

Operational 50% Rule & Worked Calculation:

Psychologists define the absolute threshold operationally as the minimum stimulation detected 50% of the time across repeated trials. If a researcher plays a faint tone \(30\) times and you detect it \(15\) times (missing it \(15\) times):

$$\frac{15}{30} \times 100 = \mathbf{50\%} \quad \text{(Your absolute threshold)}$$
Individual Variation & Response Bias:

Absolute thresholds differ across people and situations depending on physical and psychological state—including response bias (how ready a person is to report detecting a stimulus). Walking along a street at night, believing the neighborhood is dangerous makes you far more ready to detect footsteps as a threat than if you believe it is completely safe.

2 Difference Threshold, JND & Weber's Law

The difference threshold is the minimum amount of change in stimulation a person can detect between two intensities—such as adding more sugar to already-sweet coffee or slightly turning up music volume until a difference registers. Psychologists call the smallest change detectable 50% of the time the Just Noticeable Difference (JND).

Weber's Law (Proportional Constant):

Weber's Law states that the amount of change needed to produce a JND is a constant fraction (percentage) of the original stimulus intensity:

50 oz Weight → +1 oz JND
\(\frac{1}{50} \times 100 = \mathbf{2\%}\)
100 oz Weight → +2 oz JND
\(\frac{2}{100} \times 100 = \mathbf{2\%}\)

Note: Doubling the original weight doubles the required noticeable change. Weber's Law holds better for moderate-intensity stimuli than for extremely low or extremely high intensities.

3 Sensory Adaptation & Adaptive Purpose

Sensory adaptation is the tendency of sensory receptors to show decreasing responsiveness to an unchanging stimulus over time:

1. Cold Swimming Pool

Shivering initially gives way to feeling “the water is fine!”—not because the pool warmed up, but because temperature receptors adapted.

2. Garbage Smell & Train Vibration

A strong garbage smell in a friend's room or a vibrating train seat fades from conscious notice after minutes, freeing focus to detect new changes.

Protective Exception: Adaptation helps us ignore steady background noise while studying, but we do not completely adapt to extremely intense sensations—such as severe pain or freezing cold—because ignoring them could be harmful or fatal.
