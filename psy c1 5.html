Research Methods in Psychology

The Scientific Method, Descriptive, Correlational & Experimental Designs, and the Five Steps of Research

1 The Scientific Method & Core Research Terms

Psychologists cannot rely on personal opinions or guesses (e.g., assuming longer sleep automatically boosts exam scores). Instead, they use the scientific method—a systematic process of testing ideas through observation, experimentation, and statistical analysis to minimize bias and error, much like a detective building a case from evidence.

A. Theory

An integrated set of principles about observed facts intended to describe and explain aspects of experience.

B. Hypothesis

A tentative, testable prediction about the relationship between variables (e.g., “Students receiving tutorials perform better academically”).

C. Variable

Anything that can change or vary (e.g., exam scores, room temperature, gender, class size, study hours).

2 Method 1: Descriptive Research

Descriptive research records systematically observed behavior as it naturally occurs without establishing direct cause and effect. It includes three primary techniques:

A. Naturalistic Observation

Observing subjects in their real, natural environment (e.g., watching students inside an actual classroom rather than a lab).

  • Observer effect: Subjects who know they are being watched may alter behavior (becoming unusually quiet).
  • Observer bias: Researchers may focus selectively on expected behaviors (e.g., noticing aggression while ignoring friendliness).
B. Case Study

Studying one individual in great detail (e.g., someone with unusual memory). Provides deep insight, though findings cannot easily generalize to everyone.

C. Survey

Collecting data from a very large group (ideal for private/covert behaviors like exam anxiety via questionnaires). Requires a representative sample.

3 Method 2 (Correlational) & Method 3 (Experimental)

A. Correlational Research (Measuring Relationships)

Measures strength and direction of relationships without manipulating variables (e.g., Study Hours \(2,4,6,8\) vs. Exam Scores \(40,55,70,85\)).

  • Positive correlation: Both increase together (study hours & exam scores).
  • Negative correlation: One increases while the other decreases (gaming hours & study hours).
  • Zero correlation: No relationship (shoe size & exam scores).

Critical Rule: Correlation does NOT prove causation (smoking relates to lung cancer, but correlation alone reveals strength/direction, not direct causality).

B. Experimental Research (Establishing Cause & Effect)

Manipulates one factor while holding others constant to test causality (e.g., testing whether small vs. large class size improves academic performance).

4 Five Steps of Scientific Research & Final Revision

Five Ordered Steps (Violent Cartoons & Aggression Example):
  1. Defining the problem: Does watching violent cartoons affect children's aggression?
  2. Formulating the hypothesis: Children watching violent cartoons become more aggressive than those watching non-violent ones.
  3. Testing the hypothesis: Collect and compare behavioral data across both groups.
  4. Drawing conclusions: Interpret whether collected evidence supports the hypothesis.
  5. Reporting results: Document what was done, why, and what was found so others can learn.
